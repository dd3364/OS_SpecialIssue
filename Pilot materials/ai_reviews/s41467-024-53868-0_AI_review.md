## Summary

The manuscript asks whether interactional (structural) polarization and affective polarization line up across countries. It uses the complete 24-hour Twitter firehose from 21 September 2022 (Pfeffer et al.) and the Twitter Parliamentarian Database. For each of nine countries it builds a politician–retweeter bipartite network and recovers a one-dimensional latent ideology with correspondence analysis. Users are then split at zero into "left" and "right". Single-mention original posts are labelled in-group or out-group and scored with Perspective API and/or Detoxify. Main findings:

- In all nine countries, out-group mentions are more toxic than in-group mentions.
- Out-group mentions get fewer likes, and so do highly toxic posts.
- The "right" shares lower-NewsGuard-rated domains than the "left" in six countries.
- Politically engaged users show an ally–enemy pattern in quote-ratios.
- Reciprocated (dyadic) interaction is rare, especially across groups.

The core contribution is a harmonized, nine-country, seven-language replication of the "out-group interactions are more toxic" pattern, using a single complete, non-keyword dataset.

## Strengths

- **Data design.** A complete 24-hour sample, not a keyword or hashtag sample, avoids the selection problems that make cross-country Twitter comparisons hard to interpret. The paper is right to stress this.
- **Groups defined by network, not party labels.** The Liz Cheney and Tulsi Gabbard cases show why this matters. The finding that party outliers are treated as members of the opposing camp is interesting and well illustrated.
- **Consistent pipeline.** One method is applied across seven languages, with a second classifier as a robustness check where both apply, and toxicity is compared by within-country quantile. This is a sensible way to handle the fact that raw scores cannot be compared across languages.
- **Candid about limits.** The authors acknowledge where the method fits poorly (Germany's establishment–populist axis, Poland's PO/PiS split), and they discuss content moderation as an alternative explanation for the engagement results.
- **Clear headline result.** It is easy to communicate, and the multi-country framing answers a real gap: most high-profile work is US-only.

## Key claims

**1. The in-group vs out-group toxicity gap may come from who interacts across lines, not from the interaction itself. The paper cannot yet tell these apart. (Load-bearing.)**
The claim that "affective and interactional polarization align" is read as a property of out-group interactions. But the comparison pools posts across authors, and Mann–Whitney tests treat posts as independent. There is strong evidence that the users who engage across partisan lines are unusually toxic everywhere:
- Mamakos & Finkel (2023) find the bilaterally engaged are the most toxic Reddit users.
- Bor & Petersen (2022) find online hostility is driven by status-seeking individuals who are hostile in every setting.
- Kim et al. (2021) find heavy commenters are more toxic.

If a small, toxic subpopulation produces most out-group mentions, the reported gap is a composition effect. That would mean something quite different for the "alignment" claim.

*Fix, using existing data:*
- Re-estimate the gap within authors. Use mixed or fixed-effects models with author random/fixed effects, and ideally target effects too, or restrict to authors who post both in-group and out-group mentions and compare each author's own posts.
- Report how concentrated out-group mentions are: the share produced by the top 1% and top 10% of authors.
- Use clustered (by-author) bootstrap intervals in place of post-level Mann–Whitney p-values.

If the gap survives within authors, the paper's claim becomes much stronger and more novel. If it does not, that is itself a notable cross-national finding.

**2. Who is mentioned differs systematically between in-group and out-group posts. This confounds both the toxicity and the engagement results. (Load-bearing.)**
Out-group mentions are probably dominated by replies to opposing *politicians* and other prominent accounts. In-group mentions are more likely to be conversational exchanges between ordinary users or supportive quotes. Prominent targets attract more incivility regardless of partisanship (Theocharis et al. 2020 find about 18% of tweets directed at US legislators are uncivil). Post type also mechanically shapes early likes: replies buried in an opponent's thread are seen by a hostile audience, while an original post or quote is seen by the author's own followers. Conover et al. (2011) already showed that cross-ideological contact on Twitter happens mainly in the mention network, as partisans inject content into the opposing side's streams.

*Fix:*
- Stratify or control for target type (politician vs non-politician; target follower count), post type (reply / quote / original with mention), and author follower count.
- Re-run Figs. 3 and 4 within these strata.
- Add a sentence reconciling the lower out-group engagement with Rathje et al. (2021, the paper's ref. 34). That paper found out-group language *increases* engagement, but it measured words *about* the out-group in posts from news and political accounts, not mentions *of* out-group users. As written, the reader may see a contradiction.

**3. "Toxicity" is treated as a measure of affective polarization without validating it across languages or checking target/identity-term bias. (Important.)**
Affective polarization is an attitude (out-party dislike). Classifier toxicity in a mention is a noisy proxy for hostile *expression*, and several confounds matter here:
- Classifiers over-react to identity terms, swear words and dialect (Sap et al. 2019; Röttger et al. 2022 report keyword over-sensitivity and target bias across ten languages).
- Political labels used as insults, such as "fascist" or "communist", will appear far more often in out-group posts.
- Turkey uses a different model (Detoxify) from Germany and Poland (Perspective only), so cross-country "consistency" partly reflects different instruments.
- The paper says Detoxify uses "BERT sentence classifiers", but its multilingual model, the only one that handles Turkish, French, Italian and Spanish, is XLM-RoBERTa. The `original` model is BERT and English-only.

*Fix:*
- State which Detoxify checkpoint was used for each language, and report per-language agreement between Perspective and Detoxify (rank correlation) where both apply.
- Hand-annotate a small stratified sample (for example 100–200 posts per country, balanced across in-group/out-group and toxicity quantiles) to show the classifier ordering is valid in each language.
- Show the result holds with Perspective's INSULT/IDENTITY_ATTACK attributes, or after masking party names and common political slurs.
- Frame the dependent variable more cautiously ("hostile/uncivil out-group expression") and connect it to the closest conceptual precedent, Marchal (2022). Marchal found cross-partisan exchanges on r/politics more negative and more likely to end early, which also speaks to your dyadic-interaction finding.

**4. The binary left/right split mislabels key cases, and this carries into the media-reliability claim. (Important.)**
In Germany the positive-score group is AfD plus Die Linke, so "the political right reference lower reliability media" (Fig. 5, Discussion) is really a populist-vs-establishment contrast there. In Poland, PO is labelled "left". The Discussion's statement that "in each case … the political right reference lower media reliability outlets" is therefore not accurate as worded.

*Fix:*
- Use axis-neutral labels (Group A/B, or establishment/populist where appropriate), or disaggregate Germany's positive group by modal party in Fig. 5.
- Test sensitivity of all results to the split point. The zero point after rescaling peaks to ±1 need not fall at the valley of the distribution. Drop users with |score| < δ, and use the density minimum as the threshold.
- Report the minimum number of retweets needed for a user to receive a score. Users with one retweet are, in effect, labelled by a single politician.
- The media result rests on NewsGuard domains present in tweets and is barely connected to the paper's thesis. Either tie it in (for example, do toxicity and low reliability co-occur in out-group posts?) or move it to the SI. Its novelty is also overstated: Lasser et al. (2022) already compared low-quality news sharing by politicians in the US, UK and Germany, and found the partisan asymmetry was much larger in the US. That is directly relevant to a claim that the asymmetry generalizes.

**5. Novelty positioning and what the cross-national design could deliver. (Framing; high value.)**
"Existing studies are often limited to a single country" understates prior comparative work:
- Urman (2020) measured structural Twitter polarization across 16 democracies and found it is highest in two-party, plurality systems and lowest in proportional multiparty systems.
- Bor & Petersen (2022) test online political hostility across the US and Denmark.
- Survey work measures affective polarization comparatively (Reiljan 2020; Wagner 2021; Boxell et al., the paper's ref. 4).

At present, nine near-identical "yes" answers are the whole cross-national payoff. A sharper paper would use the variation:
- Does the size of the out-group toxicity gap, or the degree of structural separation (e.g., the bimodality or valley depth of the ideology distribution), track country-level affective polarization indices (Reiljan; Wagner/CSES) or electoral system?
- Even with nine countries this is descriptive, but it would turn "the pattern holds everywhere" into "the pattern holds everywhere, and its magnitude varies with X". That is a much more substantive contribution.

At a minimum, report effect sizes on an interpretable scale for each country, not only stars. Useful options are the raw difference in median toxicity score, the share of posts above 0.5, and a probability-of-superiority/Cliff's delta. With tens of thousands of posts, significance alone says little.

**6. A single day, and an unusual one. (Moderate; mostly a disclosure and robustness issue.)**
21 September 2022 is the day Russia announced partial mobilization. That is plausibly behind the German AfD/Linke alignment the authors attribute to the Ukraine-war position. It is also four days before the Italian general election of 25 September 2022. Italy's discourse was therefore in a peak-campaign state and is not comparable with the other countries.

*Fix:*
- Name these events explicitly in the limitations and in the Germany and Italy discussion.
- Where possible, show that removing posts on these topics (simple keyword filters) does not change the in-group/out-group contrasts.
- Note that a 24-hour window truncates reciprocity. The dyadic result (8.2% vs 2.2%) should be compared with a null that preserves each user's activity level, because out-group pairs may simply involve less active accounts.

## Specific comments

- **Fig. 1 / text / Methods inconsistency.** The caption says an edge requires "a common retweeter". The Results say "at least two common retweeters". The Methods say nodes are politicians "retweeted by two unique users" and edges need "the same user". Please reconcile.
- **Figure reference error.** The media-reliability paragraph says "Figure 4 shows that, for the six countries where NewsGuard…" but the result is in Fig. 5.
- **Statistics description.** The Mann–Whitney U test does not return "the probability that the two distributions are drawn from the same parent distribution". A p-value is not the probability that the null is true, and the test is sensitive to stochastic dominance, not to any difference in distribution (see Fay & Proschan 2010). Please correct the wording, and say whether p-values are adjusted for the 9 × several comparisons.
- **Bootstrap.** Resampling 50% of the data *with replacement* is non-standard. The spread of the resulting boxplots does not correspond to the sampling uncertainty of the full-sample estimate, because it is inflated by √2. Use a standard n-out-of-n bootstrap clustered by author, and show 95% CIs. Boxplots of bootstrap replicates, with "outlier" points, are an unusual way to show uncertainty. A dot-and-interval plot per country would be clearer for Figs. 3–5.
- **Fig. 3a vs 3b.** Toxicity uses differences in medians and engagement uses differences in means of log-likes. Please justify the asymmetry or harmonize.
- **Latent ideology Methods.** "Single value decomposition" should read "singular value decomposition". The text says the scores of "the retweeted users" are the row coordinates, but rows are retweeters. Say how politician positions, used for node shapes in Fig. 1, are obtained (column coordinates?). The "dummy politician" retweeted by everyone is unusual for correspondence analysis. Briefly show that results are insensitive to it, for example by restricting to the giant component.
- **Country inclusion threshold.** The "5000 unique user pairs" threshold is "determined experimentally" (note 51). Please say which countries just missed it, and show that results do not depend on it.
- **Engagement measure.** Likes collected about 10 minutes after posting capture only very early engagement. This should be in the main-text limitations, not only Methods. Reply/retweet counts at the same horizon could be shown as robustness checks.
- **Quote-ratio (Fig. 6).** Give the formula explicitly in the main text or Methods, and say how users with few mentions are handled when binning. "Most accounts are disproportionately retweeted" by both sides needs a baseline: what quote-ratio would be expected if mention types were independent of target?
- **Fig. 2 legend.** The party key mixes outdated labels (e.g., "UMP: Les Républicains"; "EM"; "FN: Rassemblement National"). Use current party names consistently.
- **Typos.** Fig. 1 caption "polarized within then"; "necesarily"; "partisan's differ".
- **Data availability.** Because the Twitter IDs are now effectively unhydratable, deposit the derived, de-identified analysis tables (ideology scores, toxicity quantiles, in-group/out-group labels, like counts per post) so the main figures can be reproduced.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Urman, A. (2020).** Context matters: political polarization on Twitter from a comparative perspective. *Media, Culture & Society* 42(6). https://doi.org/10.1177/0163443719876541. The closest prior cross-national study of structural Twitter polarization (16 countries). It undercuts the "single country" framing and offers a hypothesis (electoral system) for explaining variation in your effect sizes.
- **Bor, A. & Petersen, M. B. (2022).** The psychology of online political hostility: a comprehensive, cross-national test of the mismatch hypothesis. *American Political Science Review* 116(1). https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055421000885. A cross-national (US/Denmark) study showing that hostile individuals, not the online setting, drive political hostility. This is the central alternative explanation for Key claim 1.
- **Mamakos, M. & Finkel, E. J. (2023).** The social media discourse of engaged partisans is toxic even when politics are irrelevant. *PNAS Nexus* 2(10), pgad325. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgad325. Bilaterally engaged users are the most toxic. This directly motivates within-author analysis.
- **Kim, J. W., Guess, A., Nyhan, B. & Reifler, J. (2021).** The distorting prism of social media. *Journal of Communication* 71(6). https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqab034. Commenter self-selection and exposure effects on toxicity, a composition mechanism relevant to the in-group/out-group comparison.
- **Marchal, N. (2022).** "Be nice or leave me alone": an intergroup perspective on affective polarization in online political discussions. *Communication Research* 49(3). https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502211042516. The closest precedent for measuring affective polarization as negativity in cross-partisan exchanges, including conversation termination (relevant to your dyadic analysis).
- **Lasser, J. et al. (2022).** Social media sharing of low-quality news sources by political elites. *PNAS Nexus* 1(4), pgac186. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgac186. Prior multi-country (US/UK/Germany) evidence on partisan asymmetry in low-quality news sharing, finding it largely US-specific. This must be engaged for the Fig. 5 claim.
- **Conover, M. D. et al. (2011).** Political polarization on Twitter. *ICWSM* 5. https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v5i1.14126. The canonical demonstration that retweet networks are segregated while mention networks are where cross-ideological contact occurs. It is the conceptual basis for your design and is not cited.
- **Theocharis, Y., Barberá, P., Fazekas, Z. & Popa, S. A. (2020).** The dynamics of political incivility on Twitter. *SAGE Open* 10(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/2158244020919447. Incivility directed at legislators, relevant to controlling for target type (Key claim 2).
- **Garimella, K., De Francisci Morales, G., Gionis, A. & Mathioudakis, M. (2018).** Political discourse on social media: echo chambers, gatekeepers, and the price of bipartisanship. *WWW 2018*. https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01665. Finds bipartisan users receive less engagement, a direct precedent for your engagement result.
- **Sap, M. et al. (2019).** The risk of racial bias in hate speech detection. *ACL 2019*. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P19-1163, and **Röttger, P. et al. (2022).** Multilingual HateCheck. *WOAH 2022*. https://aclanthology.org/2022.woah-1.15. Evidence of lexical, target and dialect biases in toxicity classifiers, including cross-lingual weaknesses. These support the validation requested in Key claim 3.
- **Reiljan, A. (2020).** "Fear and loathing across party lines" (also) in Europe. *European Journal of Political Research* 59(2). https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6765.12351, and **Wagner, M. (2021).** Affective polarization in multiparty systems. *Electoral Studies* 69, 102199. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.electstud.2020.102199. Country-level survey measures of affective polarization in multiparty systems that could be set against your country-level effect sizes (Key claim 5).
- **Fay, M. P. & Proschan, M. A. (2010).** Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney or t-test? *Statistics Surveys* 4. https://doi.org/10.1214/09-SS051. Explains what the Mann–Whitney test actually tests, for correcting the Methods description.
- **Context for the observation day:** Russia's partial-mobilization announcement on 21 September 2022 (https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/09/21/npr-putin-announces-a-partial-military-mobilization-for-russian-citizens; https://kyivindependent.com/national/putin-russia-initiates-partial-mobilization) and the 25 September 2022 Italian general election (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Italian_general_election).

## Overall assessment

The dataset is unusually good for cross-national work, and the descriptive pattern is clear, consistent and worth publishing. But the central inference, that out-group *interactions* are more hostile and that this shows affective and interactional polarization "align", rests on post-level comparisons. These do not separate who interacts across lines, whom they target, and in what format, from the interaction itself. Classifier validity across seven languages and the left/right labelling (notably Germany in the media analysis) also need work.

Most fixes use data the authors already have: within-author models, stratification by target and post type, clustered uncertainty with effect sizes, and a small human validation sample. Together with tighter positioning against Urman (2020), Bor & Petersen (2022) and Lasser et al. (2022), they would make this a strong broad-audience paper, for example at *Nature Communications* or *PNAS Nexus*, with *EPJ Data Science* a natural fit if the cross-national variation analysis is not pursued.

**Recommendation: major revisions.**
