# Review: "Research Quality of Registered Reports Compared to the Standard Publishing Model"

## Summary

The manuscript asks whether Registered Reports (RRs) produce research that expert peers judge to be of higher quality than comparable work published in the standard model, and whether RRs cost anything in novelty or creativity. The authors took 29 novel, non-replication psychology and neuroscience RRs published 2014–2018. They matched each to up to two comparison articles: one from the same journal (or, for *CRSP*, from *Social Psychology*) and one by the same first, last or corresponding author. They then had 353 self-selected researchers rate one RR and one comparison article on 19 nine-point items. Reviewers read each paper in three stages (intro/methods, then results/discussion, then abstract). In a Bayesian multilevel model that pools across outcomes, RRs score higher on average on every item: 0.46 scale points overall, about 1 point on methodological and analytic rigour, and 0.13–0.22 on novelty and creativity, where the intervals include zero. Article coding shows RRs have much more open data, open materials, preregistration and sample-size justification, and larger median samples. The design is ambitious and careful, and the question matters. As written, though, the inference drawn ("RRs could improve research quality ... without costs") outruns what a partly unblinded rating of perceived quality, in a self-selected and pro-RR rater pool, can support.

## Strengths

- **A real attempt at causal leverage in a hard setting.** The two preregistered matching schemes (journal-centric and author-centric), the random choice of which comparison article each reviewer saw, and the counterbalanced order are thoughtful. The within- and between-subjects estimates agree closely (0.46 vs 0.43; Table S10 vs Figure 1), which is reassuring.
- **Staged reading protocol.** Collecting methods ratings before reviewers see results is a clever way to separate design quality from outcome-driven judgment. It mirrors the logic of RRs and of results-blind review.
- **Transparency.** The design, the analyses and the amendment are all preregistered. The deviations (the impossible difference-score specification; the switch to a pooled model) are disclosed. A second analyst reproduced the main result in OpenBUGS, and data and code are on OSF. Given that three authors work for COS, which promotes RRs, this independent reproduction and the full disclosure are important. They should stay prominent.
- **Objective coding alongside subjective ratings.** Table 1 is informative in its own right. The contrast in open data (86% vs 14–18%) and sample-size justification (26/29 vs 5–8/28–29) is striking. As noted below, it is also the key to interpreting the rating results.
- **The authors report the analyses that cut against them.** The guessed-article-type and belief-moderation analyses are exactly the right analyses to run.

## Key claims

### 1. Reviewers largely saw through the blinding, and once that is accounted for most of the effect may be expectancy or signalling rather than quality (most consequential)

The authors present identification of RR status as a "majority" result (54%). The more telling figure is that, among reviewers who ventured a guess, 190 of 228 (83%) correctly identified the RR and 192 of 222 (86%) correctly identified the comparison paper. The rating advantage is concentrated among those who guessed correctly. For reviewers who guessed neither paper correctly (n = 87), the pooled estimate is 0.10 [−0.37, 0.55] (Table S8 intercept), about a fifth of the headline 0.46. Only 14 of 19 items stay in the RR's favour in that group. The rater pool was also, on average, favourable to RRs (belief-in-improved-rigour M = 2.01 on a −4 to 4 scale). Those with the strongest beliefs show the largest advantage (0.82), and those neutral or negative show 0.24–0.45 with intervals spanning zero.

The manuscript treats this as "modest evidence" that the advantage survives non-detection. Two points push the other way:

- **Reverse direction.** Guessing is measured after treatment, so it can be caused by perceived quality ("this looks rigorous, so it must be an RR") as easily as the reverse. Conditioning on it can bias the estimate in either direction (Montgomery, Nyhan & Torres 2018). So the 0.10 is neither a clean lower bound nor a clean "pure quality" estimate. Say so explicitly, and stop describing the subgroup results as evidence that the effect holds without detection.
- **Signals are visible in the text.** The cues that let reviewers detect RRs are the features coded in Table 1: links to preregistrations, open data statements, sample-size justifications, and likely RR-specific language such as "Stage 1", "in-principle acceptance" or "pre-specified hypotheses". Reviewers in this field reward those markers. That is partly legitimate, since they are components of rigour. But it means the study cannot separate "RRs are better research" from "RRs carry more of the markers this community rates as rigorous." Field et al. (2020) is a directly relevant (and inconclusive) experiment on whether preregistration raises academics' trust in findings, and should be engaged.

**What would address it:**
- (a) Report, per item, the estimates by guess category (none/one/both) as group means, not only as contrasts. The text currently reports "one paper correctly guessed: M = 0.44", which looks like the contrast coefficient from Table S8 (0.44) rather than that group's mean (≈0.54).
- (b) List exactly which RR cues survived redaction, and code them per article.
- (c) Run an article-level decomposition: add the coded transparency markers (sample-size justification, preregistration link, open data/materials) as pair-level covariates, or estimate the RR effect within pairs where the comparison paper shares those markers. Frame this as decomposition, not confound removal, because these markers are partly mechanisms of the RR format.
- (d) Retitle and reframe: what is measured is *peer-perceived* quality, not research quality. A title such as "Peer evaluations of Registered Reports versus matched standard articles" would be accurate. "Research Quality" in the title, and "could improve research quality" in the abstract, are not.

A sharper paper may be hiding here. What did reviewers think RRs were better *at*, and is that difference carried by observable transparency and design features? That framing turns the detection problem into a finding.

### 2. "RRs outperformed on all 19 criteria" and "no costs to novelty or creativity" are artefacts of pooling and of reading absence of evidence as evidence of absence

- **The pooled model makes "all 19 positive" close to inevitable.** The headline claim (abstract, Discussion: "for none of the 19 outcome criteria did the standard model outperform RRs") rests on the post hoc model that partially pools item effects toward a common positive mean (sd across questions = 0.28, Table S5). Under that model, small or near-zero items are pulled toward 0.46. The 19 items are also highly intercorrelated (Figure S4), which suggests strong halo effects: effectively two or three dimensions, not 19 independent tests. "All 19" therefore carries far less information than it sounds.
  - **Fix:** in the main text, report the preregistered unpooled per-item estimates alongside the pooled ones (Figure S3 exists; bring it forward). Report a factor or PCA summary of the item structure. Replace "outperformed on all 19 criteria" in the abstract with something like "point estimates favoured RRs on all items; 12 of 19 credible intervals excluded zero, largest for methodological and analytic rigour."
- **"No cost" is not established.** Novelty is 0.13 [−0.24, 0.49] and creativity 0.22 [−0.14, 0.58]. These intervals are compatible with a modest cost. The Conclusion's "without costs on research importance or creativity" and the Discussion's "We found no support for these speculations" need tempering. A defensible version is "we found no evidence of a cost, and can rule out costs larger than X."
  - **Fix:** pre-state a smallest effect of interest (for example 0.3 scale points, or a standardized value) and report posterior probability mass inside a ROPE, or an equivalence test (Lakens, Scheel & Isager 2018). Also note that the RR sample excludes replications, and that early RR adopters may have deliberately chosen safe questions. The novelty comparison is therefore about RRs authors chose to run, not about what the format would do to research in general.
- **Heterogeneity across pairs is large and unreported.** The between-pair SD of the RR effect is 0.58 [0.33, 0.90] (Table S5), larger than the mean effect of 0.46. Taken at face value, about 20% of pairs would be expected to favour the comparison article, and a 95% prediction interval for a new pair runs from about −0.7 to +1.6. "RRs outperform" describes an average over a very heterogeneous set of 28 pairs.
  - **Fix:** show a forest plot of pair-level posterior effects, ideally coloured by journal and matching scheme. Report the prediction interval and the share of pairs with negative estimates. Run a leave-one-pair-out or leave-one-journal-out analysis. *Cortex* and *CRSP* contribute large blocks of pairs.

### 3. Comparison-set construction and design moderators need more scrutiny than the text gives them

- **Matching scheme and order matter, but this is buried.** In the within-subjects model, Order1 = −0.26 [−0.42, −0.11] and Match1 = −0.18 [−0.34, −0.01]. In the between-subjects model the article type × match interaction is −0.21 [−0.46, 0.04]. The Methods themselves say a match interaction "could suggest that our matching was inadequate", and an order effect could indicate reviewers rating the second paper relative to the first. Yet the main text reports neither.
  - **Fix:** report the RR effect separately for author-centric and journal-centric comparisons and for RR-first vs RR-second, and state the deviation coding (±0.5 or ±1) so readers can compute them. If the advantage is materially smaller against same-author comparisons, that is substantively important. It would point to journal-level selection (RR-offering journals' standards) rather than the format itself.
- **Several comparisons do not deliver the intended contrast.**
  - All eight *CRSP* RRs (RR05–RR12) are compared against *Social Psychology*, a different journal that itself published RRs, so the journal-centric match is not a same-journal match for over a quarter of the sample.
  - RR22's author-centric comparison is an SSRN DOI (10.2139/ssrn.2351377). That may be a working paper rather than a peer-reviewed standard-model article.
  - RR17's author-centric comparison is in the *European Journal of Human Genetics*, which raises doubts about topical comparability and about whether reviewers matched on keywords were qualified for both papers.
  - Table S4 lists 28 pairs summing to 351 reviewers. RR13 appears to have received no reviewers, yet the text says "29 RR and control article matches ... average of 12.5 reviewers" (351/28 = 12.5).

  **Fix:** audit the comparison set, report the effective number of pairs analysed, and run a sensitivity analysis dropping *CRSP* pairs and any non-journal or off-topic comparisons.
- **The "last study" asymmetry.** Ratings target the last study. In RRs this is by design the registered, usually largest, study. In multi-study standard articles it may be a small follow-up or boundary condition. Study-number matching only partly addresses this.
  - **Fix:** report the RR effect separately for single-study pairs.
- **Selection within authors.** Authors may reserve the RR route for their best-planned projects, so even same-author comparisons can favour RRs through project selection rather than through the format. The Limitations section acknowledges self-selection in general terms. This specific mechanism should be named, together with an explicit statement of the selection-on-observables assumption (Stuart 2010). It should also be reflected in the causal language: "RRs could improve research quality" in the abstract and "the higher quality of RRs is a function of three factors" in the Conclusion.

### 4. Sample representativeness and modelling choices (moderate)

- **Rater pool.** 4.5% of invitations produced a completed review. The pool was familiar with and favourable to RRs, and the recruitment email told invitees they would rate "creativity and rigor". Reviewer-level weighting or post-stratification on RR beliefs would show how much the estimate depends on the pool's priors. Please give n per belief and familiarity level; the "negative" cell is presumably tiny. Also note the low baseline reliability of peer judgments (Bornmann, Mutz & Daniel 2010). The participant SD (≈1.46) is as large as the residual SD, which is consistent with that.
- **Effect-size metric.** The 0.46 is in raw scale points on a 9-point bipolar scale. The power analysis was framed as d = 0.4. With participant SD ≈ 1.46 and residual ≈ 1.45 on the difference scores, the standardized effect is roughly 0.2. Report both so readers do not read 0.46 as d = 0.46.
- **Ordinal outcomes.** Nine-point Likert items, and differences between them, are modelled as Gaussian. Metric models of ordinal data can distort effects and even their ordering (Liddell & Kruschke 2018). A cumulative-link robustness check in brms is straightforward (Bürkner & Vuorre 2019).

## Specific comments

- **Discussion, first paragraph.** The stage-specific summaries are swapped and the ranges are wrong. From the Results values:
  - before findings: M = 0.45, range 0.13–0.99;
  - after findings: M = 0.51, range 0.28–0.96;
  - after finishing: M = 0.39, range 0.18–0.66.

  The text gives "after showing the findings (M = 0.39; Range = 0.18–0.66)" and "after finishing the paper (M = 0.51; Range = 0.28–0.97)".
- **Abstract and Results.** These disagree on the maximum effect and on analytic rigour. The abstract gives methodology 0.99 and analysis 0.97, while the Results give analysis 0.96 and "a range of 0.13 to 0.96". The maximum should be 0.99.
- **Results, "Qualification of outcomes".** "Larger performance advantage for RRs those who were slightly favorable" is missing "than".
- **Discussion.** "It also held whether reviewers had experience or familiarity" — the lowest-familiarity estimate is 0.32 [−0.24, 0.77]. Rephrase.
- **Methods, Bayesian estimation.** The priors are described both as "uninformative ... results depend only on the data" and as "weakly informative N(0, 2)". Pick one description. "The priors we use have no relevance to the results" should be "results were insensitive to the priors examined (Figure S2)".
- **Tables S5–S9.** "All Pareto K estimates were good (k < .05)" presumably means k < 0.5 (or 0.7). Tables S8 and S9 report identical LOO values (24149.9, SE 147.8) for different models fitted to different data (S9 is between-subjects, first article only). That looks like a copy error.
- **SI cross-references.** "Table S2 shows the keywords groups" should be Table S3. The power discussion cites ref. 35 (Chambers & Mellor, on protocol transparency) for between-article variance. The claim that RR acceptance rates are higher cites ref. 28, which is a citation-impact study. "Positive results are much more likely to be published^5–7" includes ref. 5 (Smith, on peer review generally). Check all SI reference numbering.
- **Table 1.** Statcheck detected tests in only 10–12 last studies per group, so the error comparison is essentially uninformative. Say so, or move it to the SI. The journal-centric sample-size mean of 1150 (SD 5119) is driven by an outlier. Report the IQR and drop the mean.
- **Figure 1.** Add the unpooled estimates as a second marker per item, and add a vertical reference line at a stated smallest effect of interest. This would show at a glance which items are robustly positive and which are pooled toward the mean.
- **Exploratory moderators.** Collapsing averaged belief items into 5- and 6-level factors by "rounding half values" is ad hoc. A continuous (for example spline) moderator would be cleaner and less sensitive to sparse cells.
- **Open-ended comments.** The free-text responses are described as "retained for exploratory purposes". Even a light content analysis of what reviewers cited as strengths and weaknesses would directly address Key claim 1 (signals versus substance).
- **Conflict of interest.** The disclosure is appropriate. Consider stating in the main text which analyses were run or reproduced by authors not employed by COS.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Field, S. M., Wagenmakers, E.-J., Kiers, H. A. L., Hoekstra, R., Ernst, A. F. & van Ravenzwaaij, D. (2020).** The effect of preregistration on trust in empirical research findings: results of a registered report. *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 7, 181351. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.181351
  - The closest experimental evidence on whether preregistration cues change academics' credibility judgments. Central to Key claim 1, because the cue, not the quality, may drive ratings.
- **Button, K. S., Bal, L., Clark, A. & Shipley, T. (2016).** Preventing the ends from justifying the means: withholding results to address publication bias in peer-review. *BMC Psychol.* 4, 59. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-016-0167-7
  - The results-free review pilot. It is the natural reference point for the staged reading design and for the "findings distract reviewers" mechanism invoked in the Conclusion.
- **Obels, P., Lakens, D., Coles, N. A., Gottfried, J. & Green, S. A. (2020).** Analysis of open data and computational reproducibility in Registered Reports in psychology. *Adv. Methods Pract. Psychol. Sci.* 3, 229–237. https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245920918872
  - Covers the same 2014–2018 RR population. It offers an objective, non-perception quality outcome (data and code availability, reproducibility) that complements Table 1 and tempers the open-data figures.
- **Wiseman, R., Watt, C. & Kornbrot, D. (2019).** Registered reports: an early example and analysis. *PeerJ* 7, e6232. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6232
  - A prior retrospective RR vs non-RR comparison (*European Journal of Parapsychology*). It should be cited where the manuscript says there is "little empirical evidence".
- **Montgomery, J. M., Nyhan, B. & Torres, M. (2018).** How conditioning on posttreatment variables can ruin your experiment and what to do about it. *Am. J. Polit. Sci.* 62, 760–775. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12357
  - Explains why subsetting on correct or incorrect RR guesses does not identify a "pure quality" effect (Key claim 1).
- **Stuart, E. A. (2010).** Matching methods for causal inference: a review and a look forward. *Stat. Sci.* 25, 1–21. https://doi.org/10.1214/09-STS313
  - A framework for stating the selection-on-observables assumption, assessing balance on coded covariates, and sensitivity analysis for the matched comparison (Key claim 3).
- **Lakens, D., Scheel, A. M. & Isager, P. M. (2018).** Equivalence testing for psychological research: a tutorial. *Adv. Methods Pract. Psychol. Sci.* 1, 259–269. https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918770963
  - Needed to turn "no evidence of cost to novelty or creativity" into a bounded claim (Key claim 2). The ROPE analogue applies directly to the Bayesian model.
- **Liddell, T. M. & Kruschke, J. K. (2018).** Analyzing ordinal data with metric models: what could possibly go wrong? *J. Exp. Soc. Psychol.* 79, 328–348. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.08.009
- **Bürkner, P.-C. & Vuorre, M. (2019).** Ordinal regression models in psychology: a tutorial. *Adv. Methods Pract. Psychol. Sci.* 2, 77–101. https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918823199
  - Together, these two motivate and implement the cumulative ordinal robustness check (Key claim 4) in the brms framework already in use.
- **Bornmann, L., Mutz, R. & Daniel, H.-D. (2010).** A reliability-generalization study of journal peer reviews. *PLoS ONE* 5, e14331. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0014331
  - Shows that inter-rater reliability of peer review is low. This contextualizes the large reviewer-level variance and argues for reporting reliability or ICCs of the ratings.

## Overall assessment

**Recommendation: Major revisions.**

This is an important and carefully executed first empirical comparison of RRs with standard articles, and its descriptive findings are valuable. These include large differences in rated methodological and analytic rigour, and in objective transparency and sample-size practices. The central interpretive claims need to be brought into line with the design. The outcome is perceived rather than actual quality. RR status was detectable to most reviewers who guessed, and the advantage shrinks to about 0.10 among those who did not detect it. "All 19 criteria" and "no cost to novelty or creativity" are products of partial pooling and wide intervals. Pair-level heterogeneity, matching-scheme and order effects, and several imperfect comparisons are not yet reported in the main text. All of this can be fixed with the existing data, through reframing, decomposition, unpooled and equivalence analyses, and a comparison-set audit. Done well, the result would be a more credible and more interesting paper about what peers see as better in RRs and why.
