# Review: "Globalization of wild capture and farmed aquatic foods"

## Summary

The manuscript introduces ARTIS (Aquatic Resource Trade in Species), a database that turns bilateral commodity trade (CEPII BACI, HS 6-digit) into estimated species-level, farmed-versus-wild, marine-versus-inland trade flows with a source country attached, for 1996–2020. For each country-year-HS version, a quadratic program solves a production–trade mass balance, estimating the share of each species going into each commodity (X), the processing of imports into other commodities (W), domestic consumption (c) and an error term (ε). These estimates are then used to give species mixes to domestic exports and to trace foreign-sourced exports back up to three steps. The paper uses the database to describe globalization: the share of production exported, partner counts, and export/import concentration. It also describes regional and bilateral flows and apparent consumption, including how much of it is foreign-sourced. The main contribution is the database itself, as an open, reproducible species- and source-resolved trade and consumption dataset. The descriptive results are secondary and mostly confirm known patterns.

## Strengths

- **A real data gap, clearly framed.** The Introduction explains well why production and trade data do not line up: species and live weight on one side, commodities and product weight on the other, with no farmed/wild flag. It also shows why one-to-many and many-to-one species–commodity links plus re-processing make a naive concordance fail. No other open global dataset gives annual species × method × habitat × source-country flows over 25 years.
- **The mass balance includes processing of imports (W).** Most earlier reconstructions ignore that imports are processed and re-exported. Given the evidence that most of China's imports are re-exported (Asche et al. 2022, ref. 33), this matters a lot for allocating flows to a source country.
- **Sensible constraints on the solution space.** Habitat-restricted taxon–code matching, rules that products only move to more processed forms, and a mass-loss cap on processing ratios all shrink the space of feasible solutions in ways that can be defended.
- **Internal consistency checks are reported.** Flows sum back to BACI, constraints are satisfied, and domestic exports do not exceed production. Code and data are archived (GitHub, Zenodo, KNB). This openness is a major asset.
- **Headline consumption totals match FAOSTAT.** Per-capita consumption is 15.7 vs 15.6 kg in 1996 and 20.0 vs 20.7 kg in 2019. This is a reassuring aggregate sanity check.

## Key claims

### 1. The species, method and source-country splits have no external validation or uncertainty. This is the central weakness.

The paper's value rests on disaggregations that the solver invents rather than observes. Inside the feasible set, the QP picks a solution by assuming "an even distribution of production among commodities unless the data suggests otherwise" and by giving priority to codes with finer species resolution. That is a maximum-entropy-style prior. The farmed/wild and species allocation inside any mixed code is therefore driven mostly by the objective function, not the data. Yet the only checks reported are internal: flows sum to BACI and constraints hold. Those checks are true by construction and say nothing about whether the species and source allocations are correct. Every result figure (Fig. 1–4) is shown as a point estimate with no uncertainty, apart from the CI on mean degree in Fig. 1c.

**What would address it:**

- **(a) Validate against independent data that is finer than HS-6.** Good sources:
  - *US NOAA Fisheries foreign-trade data.* It uses HTS-10 codes that often name the species, as well as fresh/frozen and sometimes farmed/wild (e.g., Atlantic salmon, shrimp). Hold these out and compare ARTIS's US species mixes with them.
  - *Norwegian and Chilean salmon exports,* which are essentially all farmed.
  - *EU Comext CN-8 data,* which ARTIS already uses for conversion factors through EUMOFA.

  Report agreement statistics, for example the share of mass assigned to the correct species/method per code-country.
- **(b) Check how sensitive the results are to the QP objective.** Vary the weights on the evenness, species-priority and error terms. Show how much the headline numbers move: the farmed share of exports (60% capture), the foreign share of consumption (20.1% to 20.9%), and Europe's 72% foreign-sourced consumption.
- **(c) Carry ranges through to the results.** The Methods already compute maximum, minimum and midpoint domestic/foreign export splits (Eqs 2–7), but only midpoints are used. Show these as bands in Fig. 1a,b and Fig. 4c,d.

The approach is close to the entropy-based matrix-balancing literature (Golan, Judge & Robinson 1994). Placing it there would justify the prior and point to standard ways of reporting cell-level uncertainty.

### 2. The trend claims may be artifacts of how the series is built, and the paper needs to show they are not.

The paper's main messages are about change over time: the share of production exported rose about 40%, export partners nearly doubled (21.9 to 41.5), and export concentration moved from 18 to 21 countries. Several design choices could produce trends without any real change:

- **HS version splicing.** The series switches from HS96 to HS02 in 2004, to HS07 in 2010 and to HS12 in 2013. The authors themselves note that HS12 brought "major revisions to the HS codes relevant to fisheries." Finer codes change species allocation and conversion factors, and so live-weight totals, exactly at the splice years.
- **The 0.1 t flow threshold plus disaggregation.** Splitting flows into more and finer species/method lines, combined with rising BACI reporter coverage, mechanically raises the number of nonzero country links.
- **Error exports and the "unknown" source,** whose share over time is not reported.

**What would address it:**

- Recompute Fig. 1 on a single consistent HS96 series for the whole period. Mark the splice years on all time-series figures.
- Compute partner counts on the undisaggregated BACI network, so readers can see how much of the doubling is in the raw data. For comparison, Gephart & Pace (2015) already reported a rise of more than 65% in partnerships for 1994–2012 using Comtrade, which is a direct and uncited precedent.
- Use a minimum-volume threshold (e.g., ≥100 t) for counting partners.
- Report concentration with the Herfindahl–Hirschman index alongside the "number of countries making up 75%." The latter is a coarse measure that changes in integer steps.
- Report the share of error and unknown exports by year.

### 3. Some headline numbers do not match, and the consumption and export accounts need to be reconciled.

- **The abstract's 19.4% consumption figure does not match the Results.** The abstract says global consumption "increased 19.4%." The Results give 15.7 to 20.0 kg/capita, which is +27%; the per-group numbers sum to exactly these totals. If 19.4% refers to something else (another year span, or total rather than per-capita consumption), say so.
- **The export-share increase is about 37%, not 40%.** The share of production exported is said to have risen "40%"; 15.7% to 21.5% is +37%.
- **The 2020 export volume is inconsistent.** The abstract says "nearly 60 million tonnes exported in 2020." The text gives 59.2 Mt in 2019 and a 4% fall in 2020, which is about 57 Mt.
- **Foreign-sourced consumption looks hard to square with domestic exports.** Globally, foreign-sourced consumption is 20.1% of consumption in 1996, but domestic exports are only 15.7% of production. Globally, consumption of foreign product cannot exceed domestic exports that cross borders, apart from error exports, conversion-factor asymmetries and non-food removal. Please give a global balance table for selected years showing production, domestic exports, foreign exports, error exports, non-food removal, consumption and the foreign share, so readers can see where the gap comes from.
- **The cereal comparison does not compare like with like.** The 33.5% vs FAO 36% comparison is followed by "Consequently, total exports represent an even higher percentage of production," which does not follow. Then cereals (domestic share, from Clapp 2023) are compared with aquatic foods (whose figure is unclear). Compare like with like.

### 4. Production-side measurement error goes into consumption and the farmed/wild split, but is only discussed qualitatively.

- **Inland capture.** FAO inland capture is badly under-reported. Household-survey back-calculations suggest true harvests are about 65% higher (Fluet-Chouinard et al. 2018). Because ARTIS computes consumption as production minus exports, this under-count passes straight into inland capture consumption (1.51 kg/capita) and lowers the wild share in Asia and Africa.
- **Marine capture.** Unreported marine catch (Pauly & Zeller 2016) will show up as positive ε, the "error exports."
- **The consumption cap.** Capping per-capita consumption at 100 kg and rescaling is an ad hoc fix for joint-venture and flag-attribution mismatches.

**What would address it:** State plainly that inland capture figures are lower bounds. Report the number of capped countries and the volume removed. Show where error exports are concentrated (by country and code), because the error-export species mix is set to the "lowest common taxonomic resolution" and cannot be assigned farmed/wild.

### 5. The framing undersells the database and oversells the "globalization" findings.

Many of the findings repeat known patterns: China as a re-processor, Norway/Chile in salmon, net exports from least developed countries, regional trade concentrated in Asia. The new, ARTIS-only results are mostly in passing remarks. Examples:

- The farmed/wild split in exports (capture is 60% of exports while aquaculture is about half of production).
- Re-exports making up about half of China's marine capture exports.
- The rising volume moving through intermediate countries (Supplementary Fig. 5).
- The near-flat global foreign share of consumption despite rising trade.

**What would address it:**

- Lead with these findings and put the main figures to work on them. Make Supplementary Fig. 5, on the intermediate-country and traceability burden, a main-text figure.
- Add a short "what ARTIS can and cannot be used for" box. It should say the source country is the flag state; informal trade is missing; and error flows have no species or method.
- Tone down policy statements the analysis does not test. "This finding clarifies standing debates about the orientation of aquaculture" is an example: debates about the orientation of aquaculture concern who consumes farmed fish (Belton et al. 2018), which a national-level foreign share cannot settle.
- The nutrition angle (refs. 1, 43) is the obvious high-impact use. Even one worked example would show where the database adds value, such as the nutrient-weighted foreign share for a few species groups using Hicks et al. (2019) nutrient profiles.

## Specific comments

- **Methods, Eq. 1 and the vectorization paragraph.** The symbols A$, y, D_%, D_&, V and Z are not defined, and the extracted text is garbled. Write out the full stacked system, including how X and W enter one unknown vector, and give the exact QP objective with its weights. Without this the model cannot be reproduced from the paper alone.
- **"solve_qp."** Name the package (e.g., `qpsolvers`), the backend solver, the convergence tolerances, and what happens to infeasible country-years.
- **HS splice years.** "HS96 being used for 1996–2004, HS02 for 2004–2009" double-counts 2004. Clarify.
- **Fig. 1c.** The y-axis appears to top out near 40, but the text reports 41.5. Also say what the 95% CI is over (countries?). Mean out-degree is skewed, so the median may be more informative.
- **Fig. 2.** The species labels in the bar charts are broken into word fragments in the PDF ("finned fishes ray," i.e., Actinopterygii), and the bar for this unresolved taxon is uninformative. Use common names, and show unresolved taxa separately.
- **Fig. 3b.** Each row has its own y-scale, which hides relative size. Consider a chord diagram for one year plus a growth-rate heat map.
- **Live-weight conversion factors.** Fishmeal/oil is given a single factor of 2.98 (Jackson 2009). Since fishmeal is excluded from consumption but still affects how production is allocated across commodities, report how sensitive consumption is to this value. Also, averaging CN-8 factors within HS-6 codes without weighting by trade volume can bias factors for heterogeneous codes. Consider weighting by EU trade volumes.
- **Fishmeal assignment.** Fishmeal is assumed to come entirely from whole-fish reduction, even though an estimated 27% comes from by-products. This overstates the share of production going to fishmeal and understates human consumption for small pelagics. Give at least a bounding calculation.
- **Flag-state attribution.** The paper says catch "should be recorded in the national statistics of the foreign fishing vessel," which seems to mean the flag state. Rephrase. Also cite Nash et al. (2022) more directly here, since foreign fishing is a separate channel that ARTIS does not capture.
- **Uncited source-tracing methods.** The approach of tracing source countries through intermediate exporters is closely related to Kastner et al. (2011), which underlies FAOSTAT-based trade-corrected footprints. It should be cited and contrasted (proportional re-export assumption vs. the explicit W matrix).
- **Unsourced comparison.** "On par with cereal products²⁵" needs an explicit metric and number.
- **Abstract.** "11% of global agriculture trade by value" needs a source in the main text.
- **Acknowledgements.** "JAG, JAG" is duplicated.
- **Supplementary figure naming.** Supplementary figures are cited as both "Fig S8/S9" and "Supplementary Fig. 8". Standardize.
- **Suggested venue.** Nature Communications, Nature Food or Scientific Data suit a database-plus-findings paper. If the validation in Key claim 1 is added, the descriptive findings could be shortened in favour of a data-descriptor framing, which would suit Scientific Data.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Gephart, J. A. & Pace, M. L. (2015). Structure and evolution of the global seafood trade network.** *Environ. Res. Lett.* 10, 125014. https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/10/12/125014
  - The authors' own earlier network analysis found partnerships up more than 65% (1994–2012) on commodity data. It is the natural baseline for the "export partners nearly doubled" claim and should be cited and contrasted.
- **Kastner, T., Kastner, M. & Nonhebel, S. (2011). Tracing distant environmental impacts of agricultural products from a consumer perspective.** *Ecol. Econ.* 70, 1032–1040. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolecon.2011.01.012
  - The canonical method for re-attributing bilateral trade to the country of primary production, used widely in terrestrial footprint work. It is the closest methodological analogue to ARTIS's source-country tracing and is not cited.
- **Golan, A., Judge, G. & Robinson, S. (1994). Recovering information from incomplete or partial multisectoral economic data.** *Rev. Econ. Stat.* 76(3), 541–549. https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v76y1994i3p541-49.html
  - Entropy-based recovery of under-determined flow matrices, with uncertainty measures for each cell. It gives a principled basis for the "even distribution" prior and a template for reporting uncertainty (Key claim 1).
- **Fluet-Chouinard, E., Funge-Smith, S. & McIntyre, P. B. (2018). Global hidden harvest of freshwater fish revealed by household surveys.** *PNAS* 115, 7623–7628. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1721097115
  - Finds inland catch is about 65% under-reported, which directly biases ARTIS's inland capture consumption and wild/farmed shares (Key claim 4).
- **Pauly, D. & Zeller, D. (2016). Catch reconstructions reveal that global marine fisheries catches are higher than reported and declining.** *Nat. Commun.* 7, 10244. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10244
  - Gives the scale of unreported marine catch, one of the likely drivers of ARTIS's positive error terms and error exports.
- **Hicks, C. C. et al. (2019). Harnessing global fisheries to tackle micronutrient deficiencies.** *Nature* 574, 95–98. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1592-6
  - Species-level nutrient profiles that could be joined to ARTIS for a nutrient-weighted analysis of foreign sourcing, the highest-impact use of species resolution (Key claim 5).
- **Asche, F. et al. (2022). China's seafood imports—Not for domestic consumption?** *Science* 375, 386–388. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abl4756
  - Already cited, as ref. 33. ARTIS's China estimates (about half of marine capture exports are foreign-sourced) should be compared quantitatively with its 74.9% re-export figure as a partial external check.
- **US NOAA Fisheries Foreign Trade database (HTS-10, monthly, 1975 onward).** https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/inport/item/3480
  - Species- and sometimes farmed/wild-resolved import data that can serve as held-out validation of ARTIS's US species mixes (Key claim 1).
- **FAO Food Balance Sheets of fish and fishery products (FishStat / Yearbook).** https://www.fao.org/fishery/static/Yearbook/YB2017_USBcard/root/food_balance/introduction.pdf
  - The FAO apparent-consumption series for fish, built on supply/utilization accounts. Compare with it at the country and species-group level, not only for the global per-capita total.

## Overall assessment

ARTIS is an important and much-needed resource, and the mass-balance design with processing of imports is a real step beyond earlier reconstructions. The paper's headline value depends on species, method and source-country splits that are currently shown as exact point estimates and checked only internally. The trend claims also have not been tested against artifacts from HS version splicing and network construction, and several headline numbers do not match each other.

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The fixes need no new data collection: hold-out validation against finer national data, sensitivity to the QP objective, uncertainty bands, a consistent-HS robustness check, and a global balance reconciliation. Once they are done, the paper would be a strong, highly citable contribution.
