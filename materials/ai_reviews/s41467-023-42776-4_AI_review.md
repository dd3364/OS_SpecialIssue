# Review: "Assessing the value of integrating national longitudinal shopping data into respiratory disease forecasting models"

## Summary

The manuscript asks whether store-level sales of non-prescription respiratory medicines from a national UK retailer improve forecasts of weekly registered respiratory deaths (ICD-10 J00–J99) in 314 English lower-tier local authorities (LTLAs), 17 days ahead.
- **Data.** Sales come from over 2 billion transactions (2016–2020). They are assigned to LTLAs with a Gaussian store-catchment kernel and turned into absolute, relative and nationally normalised features at 17- and 24-day lags.
- **Models.** A random forest using sales plus static demographic, deprivation, housing and land-use features and weather (PADRUS) is compared with the same model without sales (PADRUNOS) and with a two-feature baseline. The split is temporal: training to December 2018, testing January 2019 to March 2020.
- **Importance analysis.** Variable importance is assessed with permutation importance, SHAP, Model Class Reliance (MCR) and Group-MCR.
- **Results.** Over all LTLAs and weeks, sales add 0.02 in R² (0.78 vs 0.76). In 41 LTLAs without suppressed counts, restricted to winter months, the gain is 0.09–0.11. Group-MCR ranks sales second only to population age structure.

The contribution is a fine-grained, national-scale test of a long-discussed syndromic data source, with an attempt to show that the data are needed using Rashomon-set importance.

## Strengths

- **A rare, valuable dataset.** Store-level sales at national scale over four years, linked to local mortality, give a substantially finer grain than most earlier over-the-counter (OTC) sales studies. The paper is right that spatial aggregation is a plausible reason for the mixed earlier findings (e.g., Todd et al.).
- **A temporal train/test split with time-series CV for tuning.** This avoids the most common leakage error in this literature.
- **A thoughtful explanation strategy.** The paper asks whether sales are *required* across all near-optimal models, not just whether one model uses them, and combines MCR with a with/without ablation. That is the right instinct.
- **Honest headline numbers.** The paper reports the modest overall gain (0.02 R²) instead of hiding it, and discusses suppression and representativeness openly.
- **Public code** (nhsx GitHub repository).

## Key claims

### 1. The baselines omit the strongest predictors, recent deaths and existing surveillance, so the added value of sales is not yet shown (load-bearing)

None of the three models includes any autoregressive information: recent registered deaths in the LTLA, or the same week last year. Nor do they include standard surveillance signals: UKHSA/RCGP influenza-like illness (ILI) consultation rates, NHS 111 calls, or emergency department (ED) syndromic data.
- **Lagged deaths.** At a 17-day horizon, deaths registered in the latest available week are plausibly the single strongest dynamic predictor. Sales may simply be acting as a proxy for the current epidemic level.
- **The Google Flu Trends precedent.** Lazer et al. (2014) showed that GFT, a digital proxy, was beaten by a simple model using lagged official surveillance data, and that the proxy mainly added value when combined with lags.
- **Syndromic sources in England.** Morbey et al. (2020) show that existing English syndromic data improve winter forecasts mainly in atypical seasons, which is the same claim made here for sales.

Without these comparators, "sales are second only to age" (Group-MCR) and "PADRUS beats PADRUNOS" only show that sales beat weather and week-of-year as the sole dynamic inputs. That is a low bar.

**Fix:** add, using information actually available at forecast time (allowing for ONS registration and publication lags):
- lagged LTLA deaths at the latest available weeks;
- a seasonal-naive or historical-average forecast;
- at least one routinely available syndromic or ILI series at regional level.

Report the gain from sales *on top of* these. If sales still add skill, the paper's claim becomes far more convincing. If not, that is an important finding in itself.

### 2. Pooled R² across LTLAs mostly measures between-area population differences; the evaluation should target temporal skill and use proper inference (load-bearing)

A two-feature baseline (week number plus population aged over 65) already reaches R² = 0.71, and age-structure features dominate every importance analysis. This shows that pooled R² is driven by cross-sectional differences in LTLA size, not by forecasting week-to-week changes.
- **The effect is hidden.** The temporal signal that sales could contribute is a small share of total variance, which partly explains the "critical yet small gain" puzzle in Section 2.5. That puzzle is largely an artefact of the metric, not a substantive finding.
- **The test.** The Wilcoxon signed-rank test on about 20,000 LTLA-weeks treats observations as independent. Errors are strongly correlated across LTLAs within a week and within LTLAs over time, so p < 0.001 is not informative. (The footnoted confidence interval of −0.42 to −0.35 also lacks units.)

**Fixes:**
- Model and report rates per 100,000, or deviations from each LTLA's seasonal expectation.
- Report within-LTLA metrics (e.g., median per-LTLA R² or skill relative to a seasonal-naive forecast) and relative MAE or skill scores against the strongest baseline.
- Test differences with a Diebold–Mariano test using autocorrelation-robust variance, or a block bootstrap by week.
- Given the decision-making framing, report probabilistic forecasts and proper scores such as the weighted interval score (Bracher et al. 2021). Random forests can give quantile predictions.

### 3. The headline gain (+0.09 to +0.11 R²) comes from post hoc subsets of a single test winter (load-bearing)

The test period (January 2019 to March 2020) contains one respiratory season, 2019/20. Its final weeks overlap with the onset of COVID-19 and the changes in shopping behaviour that came with it.
- **The headline is a subset.** The Abstract's 0.09–0.11 is computed on 41 of 314 LTLAs (those with no suppressed weeks, which are mechanically the largest) and on hand-picked month windows. Table 2 shows eight window variants. Selecting subsets after seeing results inflates effect sizes.
- **The winter-gain claim is not shown out of sample.** The 2017/18 H3N2/B season, offered as the key example of sales adapting to "feature drift", lies in the training period, so it says nothing about out-of-sample robustness.

**Fixes:**
- Use rolling-origin evaluation: refit each year and forecast each subsequent season (2017/18, 2018/19, 2019/20). Report season-by-season results, as is standard in multi-season influenza forecasting assessments (Reich et al. 2019).
- Pre-specify a single winter definition, such as epidemiological weeks 40–20, or report all windows with a multiplicity adjustment.
- Report the 41-LTLA analysis as a sensitivity analysis, not as the Abstract headline.
- Exclude or flag weeks from mid-March 2020 onward.

### 4. Suppression is handled in a way that biases both models and the comparison

Twenty to twenty-five percent of observations were set to 5 when the true count was between 1 and 4 (or 0 to 4; the text is unclear). These values are treated as exact targets and scored as such.
- The claim that suppression "simplifies the task to the advantage of models without sales data" is asserted, not shown.
- Restricting to unsuppressed LTLAs changes the population (large, urban, high retailer density), which confounds suppression with store coverage. Sales features are less noisy precisely in these LTLAs.

**Fixes:**
- Treat suppressed values as interval-censored (e.g., a censored Poisson or negative binomial likelihood, or score only uncensored weeks within all LTLAs).
- Alternatively, aggregate to a spatial unit where suppression is rare (e.g., upper-tier LAs or ICSs) as a robustness check.
- Report gains stratified by store density, which separates "less suppression" from "better sales signal".

### 5. MCR results are over-interpreted and under-specified

- **Missing definitions.** The Rashomon set depends on the tolerance ε, the reference model and the loss. None is reported, nor are the units of the MCR values.
- **Incompatible statements.** "No models within the Rashomon set can achieve their predictive scores without this set of variables" sits alongside PADRUNOS reaching R² = 0.76 without sales. Reconcile these explicitly.
  - MCR⁻ is the minimum reliance among models within ε of the PADRUS reference loss. It says nothing about models with slightly higher loss, so an ε smaller than the 0.02 R² gap makes "essential" true by construction.
  - Report ε, show how MCR⁻ for the sales group changes as ε grows, and relate it to the PADRUNOS gap.
- **Correlated features.** Permutation-based reliance is known to be distorted by correlated features. The sales features (absolute, ratio and multiplier, across categories and lags) are highly collinear. This should be discussed, and conditional permutation considered (Fisher et al. 2019 discuss this).
- **Uncited extensions.** Group-MCR for regressors is presented as a first application. The extension should be described in enough detail to reproduce, or cited.

## Specific comments

- **Abstract.** It states that sales models "significantly out-perform" others, with gains of R² 0.09–0.11 "in periods of maximum risk". Revise to state the overall gain (0.02) and that the larger gain is in a 41-LTLA winter subset.
- **Section 2.1 / Table 1.** The forecast horizon (17 days) was chosen on the national test set of a different period. That is acceptable as exploration, but say so explicitly.
  - Table 1's minimum (828) disagrees with the text (868).
  - The local models then find 24-day lags more important than 17-day ones. Consider testing horizons directly in the local model.
- **Forecast horizon and operational value.** The horizon is defined relative to *registration* date. Registration lags the date of death (median about 4–5 days in recent ONS data, longer for coroner cases), and ONS weekly data are published with a further lag.
  - State the effective lead time over when deaths become known through official channels.
  - Consider modelling date of occurrence.
- **Equation (4).** As typeset, the sum sits outside the fraction, which does not give a share of sales. Please correct it.
  - The notation also switches between LTLA and "LSOA".
  - The catchment radii are expert-chosen. Add a sensitivity analysis on bandwidth (e.g., ×0.5, ×2) and on using the store's own LTLA.
- **Footnote 10.** The influence threshold of 3.449937e-318 is effectively the double-precision underflow limit, not a substantive threshold. Rephrase.
- **Section 4.3.1.** "Independent" and "dependent" variable are swapped. "Local Tier Local Authorities" should be "Lower Tier". "SHapley Addictive" should be "Additive". "NDCR" should be "NCDR". References to "Table 1" (p. 9, p. 10) should point to Table 2.
- **Section 2.3.** SHAP was computed with KernelExplainer on samples of 100 and 1,000 points. TreeSHAP is exact and fast for random forests and would remove sampling noise.
- **Fig. 3.** Plot test-period forecasts with uncertainty intervals and observed values for several representative LTLAs, not only in aggregate, and mark the training/test boundary.
- **Representativeness.** Report store density per LTLA and test whether gains vary with it. The Discussion's claim that single-retailer results are a "lower bound" is plausible but untested.
- **Pandemic crossover.** The final weeks (March 2020), in which PADRUS predicts a rise, coincide with panic buying. Present this as anecdotal, or remove it.
- **Data access.** The commercial data cannot be shared, so release aggregated, de-identified LTLA-week sales features (or synthetic equivalents) so that the modelling can be reproduced.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Vergu et al. (2006), "Medication sales and syndromic surveillance, France," *Emerging Infectious Diseases* 12(3): 416–421.** https://doi.org/10.3201/eid1203.050573. A close precedent: OTC sales forecast ILI one to three weeks ahead nationally and regionally, with real-time validation in a held-out season. A model for both positioning and evaluation design. It is not currently cited.
- **Morbey et al. (2020), "Can syndromic surveillance help forecast winter hospital bed pressures in England?," *PLoS ONE* 15: e0228804.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0228804. English syndromic data improve winter forecasts mainly in atypical seasons, the same claim made here. Provides the relevant existing-surveillance comparator (Key claim 1).
- **Lazer et al. (2014), "The parable of Google Flu: traps in big data analysis," *Science* 343: 1203–1205.** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1248506. Shows that digital proxies drift and should be benchmarked against, and combined with, lagged official data (Key claims 1 and 3).
- **Reich et al. (2019), "A collaborative multiyear, multimodel assessment of seasonal influenza forecasting in the United States," *PNAS* 116: 3146–3154.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1812594116. The standard for multi-season, out-of-sample evaluation against seasonal baselines, including how reporting delays affect accuracy.
- **Diebold & Mariano (1995), "Comparing predictive accuracy," *Journal of Business & Economic Statistics* 13(3): 253–263.** https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1995.10524599 (record: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bes:jnlbes:v:13:y:1995:i:3:p:253-63). The appropriate test for comparing forecasts with autocorrelated errors, replacing the Wilcoxon test.
- **Bracher et al. (2021), "Evaluating epidemic forecasts in an interval format," *PLoS Computational Biology* 17: e1008618.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008618. A proper scoring rule (WIS) for probabilistic forecasts, more relevant to healthcare planning than R².
- **Roberts et al. (2017), "Cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical, or phylogenetic structure," *Ecography* 40: 913–929.** https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881. Supports blocked (spatial and temporal) validation and warns that structured data promote reliance on non-causal predictors, relevant to the static-feature dominance.
- **Fisher, Rudin & Dominici (2019), "All models are wrong, but many are useful," *JMLR* 20(177).** https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v20/18-760.html. The original MCR paper. Use it to state the ε-dependence and the limits imposed by correlated features (Key claim 5).
- **ONS, "Impact of registration delays on mortality statistics in England and Wales: 2019".** https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/deaths/articles/impactofregistrationdelaysonmortalitystatisticsinenglandandwales/2019. Needed to translate the registration-based 17-day horizon into real lead time.

## Overall assessment

The dataset is unusual and valuable, and the question is worth answering. But the current analysis does not yet show that shopping data add forecasting value beyond what is already available.
- The comparators leave out lagged deaths and existing surveillance.
- The metric (pooled R²) is dominated by population size.
- The inference ignores dependence between observations.
- The headline gain comes from post hoc subsets of a single test winter.

All of this can be fixed with the existing data, through stronger baselines, rate-based and within-area skill metrics, rolling multi-season evaluation, censoring-aware handling of suppression, and transparent MCR settings. If sales still add skill after these changes, the paper will be considerably stronger and more credible.

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The core claim of added value is plausible but not yet demonstrated against appropriate baselines and evaluation standards. Once revised, it would suit *Nature Communications*, *PLoS Computational Biology* or *Epidemics*.
