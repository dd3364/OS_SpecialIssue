# Review: "The non-independence of nations and why it matters"

## Summary

The paper argues that cross-national regressions in economics and psychology routinely ignore the fact that nations are spatially and culturally (phylogenetically) related, which inflates false-positive rates. It makes this case in four steps:

1. Bayesian multilevel models show strong geographic and linguistic "signal" in the Human Development Index (HDI) and in Inglehart's two value dimensions.
2. A review of the 100 most-cited cross-national studies of economic development and cultural values (4,308 analyses) finds that controls for non-independence are rare: 5% and 1% of analyses use them. Where controls appear, they are mostly region fixed effects, latitude or colonial history.
3. A simulation with 236 nations shows that common fixes (latitude/longitude, continent or language-family fixed effects, Conley SEs) leave false-positive rates high. Bayesian models with a geographic Gaussian process (GP) and/or linguistic covariance do much better.
4. A pre-registered reanalysis of 12 published relationships finds that 6 lose "significance" once both kinds of proximity are modelled.

The contribution is a timely, cross-disciplinary demonstration that Galton's problem remains largely unaddressed in national-level social science, together with an accessible modelling recipe.

## Strengths

- **A real and under-appreciated problem.** The paper ties it together convincingly: prevalence (review), consequences (simulation) and stakes (reanalysis). Few papers combine all three.
- **Pre-registration.** The 12 reanalyses were registered before any control models were run (OSF, 25 Jan 2022), which guards against selective reporting.
- **Two kinds of dependence.** Spatial and linguistic dependence are modelled jointly. Figure 1 and the Supplementary Results show that they are separable and both matter, which goes beyond the spatial-only critiques in economics.
- **Openness.** Code and data are on OSF with a `targets` pipeline, and the brms recipe makes adoption plausible.
- **Clear figures.** Figures 3–5 are easy to read. Figure 6, linking the strength of autocorrelation to attenuation, is a nice explanatory addition.

## Key claims

**1. The simulation tests a standard-error problem, but the paper's framing (Fig. S1) is about confounding, and spatial random effects do not reliably fix confounding.** In the generative model, x and y are drawn independently, each with autocorrelated noise. The OLS slope is therefore unbiased: its expectation is zero, and the "false positives" come purely from understated uncertainty, i.e. too few effective degrees of freedom. Supplementary Figure S1, however, motivates the problem as an unobserved common cause U that is structured by geography and language. That is a bias problem, and it is a different one. The spatial-statistics literature shows that adding a spatially structured random effect can remove, keep or even worsen confounding bias. The outcome depends on the relative spatial scales of the covariate and the confounder (Paciorek, 2010). It can also distort the fixed effect of interest even without confounding (Hodges & Reich, 2010). Neither paper is cited, and engaging with them would change how the GP/linguistic models are sold. They are a good correction for non-independent errors, not a general cure for "spurious relationships".
*Fix:*
- Add a second simulation arm with a shared latent spatial/phylogenetic factor U that drives both x and y, and report bias as well as false-positive rates for every method.
- Add an arm with a true non-zero effect where x is spatially smooth, to show what the random-effect models do to real effects.
- Reframe the Discussion so that "accounting for non-independence" (inference) and "controlling for confounding by shared history or location" (identification) are kept distinct.

**2. The simulation favours the Bayesian models because they are partly the true data-generating model, and power is never shown.** The linguistic-covariance model uses exactly the Σ used to simulate the data, so it is correctly specified. It is unsurprising that it "completely eliminates false positives" while frequentist methods that ignore Σ do not. The comparison mixes up two things: whether a method models the right covariance structure, and whether it is Bayesian or frequentist. A frequentist GLS or PGLS model with the same Σ, or a Conley/HAC estimator using the true kernel, would probably do just as well. Without that, readers may wrongly conclude that Bayesian estimation is the key ingredient.
Separately, the geographic Σ is built as 1 − scaled log distance between capitals. That implies very long-range correlation, with substantial dependence even at intercontinental distances. This regime is especially hostile to Conley SEs with finite cutoffs, and it is unclear whether it is realistic. Please also state whether that matrix is positive definite and how any non-PSD issues were handled.
Finally, only false-positive rates are reported. A method that never rejects would score perfectly.
*Fix:*
- Add (a) GLS/PGLS with the true Σ and (b) misspecified Bayesian models: the wrong kernel, or a linguistic matrix from a perturbed tree.
- Vary the decay of the geographic kernel, e.g. exponential with a range of a few hundred to a few thousand km, alongside the log-distance version.
- Report power and coverage under non-null effects. The paper's recommendation depends on the trade-off between false positives and false negatives.

**3. The reanalysis treats "the 95% CI now includes zero" as "spurious", which the evidence does not support.** Losing significance after adding a flexible random effect can mean three things: (a) the original relationship was confounded, (b) the original SEs were too small, or (c) the model has absorbed real signal and lost power. This is especially likely with n = 28–45 nations (Gelfand, Knack & Keefer, Beck et al. 2005) and spatially smooth predictors such as settler mortality or wheat-sugar suitability. Phrases such as "spurious relationships in the published literature" and "failing to replicate" overstate the case. Several attenuations in Fig. 5 are modest, and the difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself significant (Gelman & Stern, 2006).
*Fix:*
- Report for each reanalysis the posterior shift in the slope, the posterior probability that the slope has the original sign, and the ratio of posterior SD to naive SE. This separates attenuation from loss of precision.
- Add a placebo test in the style of Kelly (2019): replace the predictor with simulated spatial/phylogenetic noise that has the same autocorrelation as the original predictor, and report how often the naive model "finds" an effect for each dataset. This directly calibrates how fragile each published result is.
- Drop "spurious" in favour of "not robust to modelling non-independence".

## Specific comments

- **"Over 200 years" (Abstract, Introduction).** The anthropological recognition cited is Galton's comment on Tylor (1889), which is about 130 years before this paper. Correct the claim or support it with an earlier source.
- **Economics literature on relatedness.** The paper underplays work in economics that models deep relatedness between countries explicitly, notably Spolaore & Wacziarg's (2009) use of genetic and ancestral distance in bilateral income regressions. The persistence critique (Kelly) is cited only via the 2020 "Understanding persistence" paper. Kelly's (2019) "Standard errors of persistence" is the most direct precedent for your simulation and should be engaged. Acknowledging these makes the contribution more precise: the novelty is the joint linguistic + spatial treatment and the review of practice, not the observation itself.
- **Anthropology/ecology method reviews.** Cite Mace & Pagel (1994) as the canonical statement of phylogenetic methods for Galton's problem. Cite Dormann et al. (2007), whose simulation compared a wide range of spatial-autocorrelation methods (GLS, autoregressive models, eigenvector filtering, GEE), as precedent and to justify which alternatives you omit. The authors' own Matthews et al. (2016) applies linguistic affiliation to national political and economic change and is a natural precedent.
- **Systematic review coding.** All coding was done by the first author. Report inter-rater reliability on a random subset coded by a second person. Analysis-level proportions are dominated by papers with hundreds of analyses (e.g., robustness tables). The article-level numbers in Fig. 2a are the more meaningful headline, so lead with them. Also clarify whether clustering SEs by region, or spatial-lag/SAR models, were counted as controls.
- **Sampling frame of the review.** The frame is Web of Science keyword searches covering 1900–2018, ranked by citations per year. Keyword-based selection may under-represent economics papers that do model spatial dependence but do not use "cross-national/cross-country" in the abstract. Acknowledge this possible bias.
- **Figure 1 / signal estimates.** The HDI and values data are longitudinal, so "proportion of national-level variance" is computed after pooling waves. State how time was handled (year effects? nation × year?) and whether the signal is stable across waves.
- **Linguistic proximity matrix.** It is built from an undated, unresolved Glottolog tree using node counts. Node depth varies arbitrarily across families, so proximities are not comparable between families. Provide a sensitivity analysis, e.g. an alternative proximity measure or a coarser family/genus-only matrix, and justify the Brownian-motion assumption.
- **Geographic GP.** The GP uses squared Euclidean distance on raw latitude/longitude, which distorts distances at high latitudes and across the antimeridian (e.g., Pacific nations, Fiji/Tonga vs. Americas). Use great-circle distances or 3-D Cartesian coordinates. Also note that brms supports only the squared-exponential kernel, which is very smooth. An exponential/Matérn kernel via Stan or INLA would be a useful robustness check.
- **Figure 6.** The regressions use n = 12 with posterior medians on both axes and ignore uncertainty in both. Present the plot as descriptive, or propagate the posterior draws.
- **Reference 28.** Jarque & Bera (1987) is a normality test and does not support the claim that residuals must be independently and identically distributed. Use a standard regression text instead. Reference 30 has a garbled author field ("Roberts, J., Seán AND Winters").
- **Practical guidance.** The Discussion would benefit from a short decision box for applied researchers: when to use GLS/PGLS, a GP or a SAR model, how to report a Moran's I on residuals, and what to do with small n.

## Literature and data to engage

- Paciorek, C. J. (2010). The importance of scale for spatial-confounding bias and precision of spatial regression estimators. *Statistical Science* 25(1). https://doi.org/10.1214/10-STS326. Shows that spatial random effects reduce confounding bias only under specific scale conditions; central to Key Claim 1.
- Hodges, J. S. & Reich, B. J. (2010). Adding spatially-correlated errors can mess up the fixed effect you love. *The American Statistician* 64(4). https://doi.org/10.1198/tast.2010.10052. Explains why adding a spatial random effect can shift or absorb the coefficient of interest; bears on interpreting Fig. 5.
- Kelly, M. (2019). The standard errors of persistence. CEPR DP13783. https://cepr.org/publications/DP13783. The direct precedent for the simulation logic (spatial noise regressions) and a ready-made placebo diagnostic for the reanalyses.
- Spolaore, E. & Wacziarg, R. (2009). The diffusion of development. *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 124(2). https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2009.124.2.469. Economics work that explicitly models deep ancestral relatedness between nations; it should be acknowledged and contrasted with your phylogenetic approach.
- Mace, R. & Pagel, M. (1994). The comparative method in anthropology. *Current Anthropology* 35(5). https://doi.org/10.1086/204317. The canonical argument for phylogenetic methods to solve Galton's problem, which the paper's anthropological framing should rest on.
- Dormann, C. F. et al. (2007). Methods to account for spatial autocorrelation in the analysis of species distributional data: a review. *Ecography* 30(5). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2007.0906-7590.05171.x. A simulation comparison of many spatial methods (GLS, SAR, eigenvector filtering, GEE); a precedent for your design and a guide to the omitted alternatives.
- Matthews, L. J., Passmore, S., Richard, P. M., Gray, R. D. & Atkinson, Q. D. (2016). Shared cultural history as a predictor of political and economic changes among nation states. *PLOS ONE* 11(4). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152979. Earlier evidence that linguistic affiliation predicts the diffusion of national political and economic change, supporting the use of linguistic proximity.
- Gelman, A. & Stern, H. (2006). The difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself statistically significant. *The American Statistician* 60(4). https://doi.org/10.1198/000313006X152649. Relevant to counting reanalyses that "lose significance" as failures.

## Overall assessment

This is an important, well-executed and clearly written paper on a problem that deserves wide attention, and the review and pre-registered reanalyses are valuable contributions. The central inferential story needs reworking. The simulation shows inflated false-positive rates from correlated errors under a regime that favours the correctly specified Bayesian models, but the paper presents the result as a solution to confounding. It also equates loss of significance with spuriousness and reports no information on power. Adding a confounding arm, misspecified and GLS comparators, and power analyses, together with the spatial-confounding literature, would make the paper much more convincing to the econometricians it aims to persuade. Most of this can be done with the existing pipeline. **Recommendation: major revisions** (analysis and framing; no new data collection needed). The paper fits *Nature Communications* or *Nature Human Behaviour*.
