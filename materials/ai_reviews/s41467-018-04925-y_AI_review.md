# Review: "Reciprocity of social influence"

## Summary

The paper asks whether people take more advice from those who take advice from them. Participants located a briefly flashed visual target, saw a simulated partner's estimate, and then alternated between revising their own estimate and watching the partner revise theirs. The partner's susceptibility to the participant's estimate was manipulated. The partner's first estimates came from the same distribution in every condition.

- **Experiment 1 (N = 17 after exclusions):** participants took more influence from a susceptible partner than from an insusceptible or baseline partner, presented in separate blocks.
- **Experiment 2A (N = 30):** the same effect appeared within a single partner whose susceptibility switched midway.
- **Experiment 2B (N = 30):** the effect was absent when the partner was described as a computer.
- **Self-ratings:** participants rated their own performance lower after working with an insusceptible partner.

The claimed contribution is that advice-taking follows a reciprocity norm that goes beyond reliability-weighted information integration. On this account, reciprocal influence works as a social signal.

## Strengths

- **A clean, under-studied question.** Advice-taking is almost always studied one-way, in a judge–advisor setup. Making influence bidirectional and manipulating the partner's susceptibility while holding the partner's accuracy fixed is a neat design. It speaks to both the advice-taking and the reciprocity literatures.
- **Partner accuracy is held constant across conditions.** This rules out the most obvious reliability account by construction. The authors also check actual and perceived partner precision, confidence and liking as covariates.
- **Three converging designs.** A between-partner design, a within-partner switch, and a human-versus-computer framing give converging evidence. The interaction in Experiment 2A/B is the most theoretically informative result.
- **A result that cuts against the informational account.** Participants rated themselves lower in the insusceptible condition, yet took less advice there. A purely informational account predicts the opposite: lower self-confidence should increase advice-taking. As discussed below, this is a strength the authors have not yet used.

## Key claims

### 1. The human-vs-computer contrast does not cleanly isolate "beliefs about the partner's mind"

**The claim and why it matters.** Experiment 2B carries the paper's theoretical claim: that reciprocity is a "signaling medium for human-to-human communication" and depends on "whether we believe it changes the partner's state of mind". Two problems weaken this inference.

**Problem 1: the computer framing changes how informative the partner's revisions are.**
- Participants took more influence from the computer overall (.42 vs. .34). This suggests they credited the "computer" with different competence.
- For a computer, a revision toward the participant is easily read as a property of the algorithm. A computer that "defers" to a human may look like a weak algorithm, which predicts less weight on a susceptible computer.
- The non-significant reversal in Experiment 2B (Z = −1.57) fits this competence-inference account at least as well as the absence of a mind to signal to.
- People's trust in algorithms depends heavily on what they observe the algorithm doing (Dietvorst et al. 2015).

**Problem 2: Experiments 2A and 2B appear to be separate experiments, not randomly assigned arms.** The Methods say each participant took part in only one "experiment", and nothing indicates concurrent random assignment. The between-subject interaction is therefore exposed to differences in recruitment and timing.

**What would address it:**
- State explicitly whether assignment to 2A or 2B was random and concurrent.
- Report end-of-study ratings of partner competence and partner "intentionality" separately for 2A and 2B. Test whether the reciprocity × partner-type interaction survives adjustment for perceived competence of the computer.
- In the Discussion, give the competence-inference alternative a fair hearing and temper the "signaling" language accordingly.
- Relate the design to classic human-versus-computer partner controls in social decision-making, such as Sanfey et al. (2003). There, the same behaviour from a computer elicited weaker social responses, but differences in perceived intentionality were explicitly at issue.

### 2. The data permit a much sharper, trial-level test of reciprocity than the block averages currently reported

**Why the current tests fall short.** All the main tests compare condition means. Yet the partner's susceptibility varied randomly from trial to trial within conditions:
- In the susceptible condition it was a mixture: 50% from U[.7, 1], 20% from U[.3, .7], 30% from U[0, .3].
- In the insusceptible condition it was drawn from U[0, .3].
- The transition block in Experiment 2A was a linear ramp.

That built-in randomness is a gift. Regress each participant-revision trial's influence on the partner's realized influence on the preceding partner trial(s), with lags 1–3, using a mixed model with random slopes per participant and condition as a covariate. This tests reciprocity as a contingent, trial-by-trial response rather than a block-level disposition. It also directly supports the claim in Experiment 2A that reciprocity is "a dynamic process", which is currently backed only by a susceptible-minus-insusceptible block contrast.

**What would also help:**
- Plot influence across blocks in Experiment 2A, including the transition block. Show how fast participants adapt and whether adaptation differs by switch direction.
- Test the order × condition interaction. Condition is confounded with time within each participant, and counterbalancing alone does not remove fatigue or learning effects that are asymmetric between orders.
- Fit a simple learning model in which advice weight is updated by a reciprocity prediction error. Compare it with an informational model in which advice weight tracks inferred partner reliability. This would turn a descriptive effect into a mechanistic one, which suits Nature Communications.

### 3. Informational alternatives are dismissed too quickly, but the authors' own data can argue against them more convincingly

**The objection.** A sceptical reader will argue that the partner's revision is informative. A partner who defers might be inferred to be better calibrated, or to have rightly judged the participant's estimate as good. Either inference could shift the participant's weighting without any reciprocity norm. The covariate models (perceived precision, liking, confidence) are only a partial answer:
- They use one end-of-experiment rating per partner.
- Experiment 1 has only 17 participants.
- In the trial-level model, the effect of condition drops to p = .07 when the confidence × condition interaction is included.

**A stronger argument the authors already have.** Self-rated performance was lower in the insusceptible condition (Fig. 3E). Under any reliability-weighting account, lower self-assessed accuracy should increase reliance on the partner. Participants did the opposite. This dissociation is the most convincing evidence against a purely informational account, but the paper presents it only as a downstream "self-image" effect.

**What would address it:**
- Make the dissociation argument explicit. Test it at the participant level: does the drop in self-rating predict the drop in influence, or run against it?
- Analyse whether trial-level confidence differs between conditions. The paper reports only that confidence is correlated across conditions (slope ≈ .8), which does not answer this.
- Engage the reputation-formation literature in advice-taking, in which advisors who agree with one's own view gain weight over time (Yaniv & Kleinberger 2000). Show that agreement on partner trials, which is mechanically higher with a susceptible partner, does not account for the effect.
- Discuss the emotion route. Being ignored is aversive, and incidental anger lowers advice-taking (Gino & Schweitzer 2008). This is a plausible proximal mechanism that is neither purely informational nor strategic signalling.

### 4. Statistical reporting has errors, and the samples are small for some claims

**Reported statistics that do not match.**
- In the two-way ANOVA, F(1,58) = 14.8 corresponds to p ≈ .0003, not .00001.
- F(1,58) = .49 corresponds to p ≈ .49, not .51.
- In Experiment 1, Z = 2.34 gives an uncorrected two-sided p ≈ .019, which is ≈ .04 after doubling for Bonferroni, not .03. State how many comparisons were corrected for. Insusceptible vs. baseline appears to be reported uncorrected.
- Mean errors are reported as "67 ± 7 radians" and "64 ± 6 radians". These values are impossible in radians. Please give the correct units (pixels? degrees?) and describe the geometry. "Angular displacement" implies targets on a circle around fixation, but the stimulus description does not say so.
- The repeated-measures ANOVA degrees of freedom (F(2.49, 39), F(1.7, 27)) are inconsistent with 17 participants and three conditions. Check the Greenhouse–Geisser corrections.

**Partner parameters do not match their generative description.**
- The insusceptible partner's influence is said to be drawn from U[0, .3], which has a mean of .15. Yet the realized mean is reported as .30 in Experiment 1 and .07 in Experiment 2A, even though the generative procedure was supposedly the same.
- The susceptible mixture has an expected value of about .57. The reported realized means are .55 and .50.

Clarify whether the algorithm differed between experiments, and how "influence" was computed for the partner. This matters because the manipulation in Experiment 1 (.55 vs. .30) is much weaker than in Experiment 2A (.50 vs. .07).

**Sample size.**
- Experiment 1 has N = 17 and no power justification.
- The between-group interaction in Experiment 2A/B rests on 30 per group.
- The trial-level mixed models report t-tests with about 4,000 degrees of freedom. Degrees of freedom should reflect the number of participants (for example, Satterthwaite or Kenward–Roger).

**What would address it:** correct the statistics, report effect sizes with confidence intervals for all main contrasts, and justify the sample sizes, or state the limitation explicitly.

## Specific comments

- **Measure naming.** The influence measure is the standard "weight of advice" (WOA) used in the judge–advisor literature (Yaniv 2004; Bonaccio & Dalal 2006). Name it as such, and relate the mean of .36 to typical egocentric-discounting values. Describe how trials with a very small initial participant–partner distance were handled, since the ratio is unstable there. Also say whether values were truncated to [0, 1].
- **High-confidence trials.** On trials where participants reported high confidence (5–6), the partner's first estimate was drawn uniformly within ±20° of the participant's own choice. Partners therefore agreed systematically when participants were confident. This applies equally across conditions, but it changes the meaning of "partner accuracy" and the ratio measure on those trials. Report the effect separately for high- and low-confidence trials, or excluding the high-confidence trials.
- **Baseline condition.** The baseline differs structurally: the participant revises on every trial and never observes the partner revising. It therefore differs in workload, number of revision opportunities, and the information available, not only in susceptibility. Acknowledge this when interpreting susceptible > baseline. The susceptible vs. insusceptible contrast is the clean test and should be primary.
- **Belief checks.** Experiment 1 reports that all participants believed the partners were human. Report the equivalent for Experiments 2A and 2B, especially that 2B participants believed the partner really was a computer and was not human-controlled. Say how the self and partner performance ratings were collected per condition in Experiment 2, given that a single partner was used.
- **Exclusions.** Report exclusions for Experiments 2A and 2B. The total is given as 80 participants (20 + 30 + 30), but Experiment 1 excluded 3.
- **Discussion.** "Being ignored exerts a devastating impact on self-efficacy" overstates a difference in one end-of-study rating. Use calibrated language.
- **Discussion, equality bias.** This section is speculative and long relative to its connection with the data. Shorten it, or turn it into a concrete prediction.
- **Abstract and significance statement.** The claim that reciprocity aims at "maintaining a positive self-image" is not tested. The self-rating effect could equally reflect an inference about accuracy, as the authors themselves note. Phrase it as a hypothesis.
- **Prior work by the senior authors' group.** Hertz et al. (2017) showed that advisers strategically manage their influence over clients, which is the mirror image of the present question. It should be discussed as the closest prior work on influence as a strategic social quantity.
- **Reference relevance.** Refs. 16 (audit-task justifiability) and 17 (person–organization fit in marketing) do not appear to support the claims about social information improving evaluative judgement. Please check them.
- **Data availability.** "Upon request" falls short of current journal norms. Deposit the trial-level data and analysis code in a public repository.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Hertz, Palminteri, Brunetti, Olesen, Frith & Bahrami (2017), "Neural computations underpinning the strategic management of influence in advice giving," *Nature Communications* 8, 2191.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02314-5
  Shows that people track and strategically manage their influence over others. This is the advice-giver side of the reciprocity loop, and it undercuts the claim that no study has examined how we "receive and inflict" influence.
- **Yaniv & Kleinberger (2000), "Advice taking in decision making: Egocentric discounting and reputation formation," *OBHDP* 83, 260–281.** https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.2000.2909
  Advisors' reputations, and hence their weights, are updated from experience. This is the main informational alternative that the reciprocity account must outcompete.
- **Bonaccio & Dalal (2006), "Advice taking and decision-making: An integrative literature review," *OBHDP* 101, 127–151.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2006.07.001
  Standard reference for the WOA measure and for judge–advisor findings, including the role of relationship factors in advice weighting. It positions the paper within the field it most directly extends.
- **Soll & Larrick (2009), "Strategies for revising judgment: How (and how well) people use others' opinions," *JEP:LMC* 35, 780–805.** https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015145
  Sets out the normative benchmarks (averaging versus choosing) against which the "deviation from reliability weighting" claim should be quantified.
- **Gino & Schweitzer (2008), "Blinded by anger or feeling the love: How emotions influence advice taking," *J. Appl. Psychol.* 93, 1165–1173.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.5.1165
  Anger reduces and gratitude increases advice-taking. This offers a concrete affective mechanism for the insusceptible effect, and could be tested with a brief affect probe.
- **Pescetelli, Rees & Bahrami (2016), "The perceptual and social components of metacognition," *JEP:General* 145, 949–965.** https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000180
  Shows that social context shapes confidence in the same kind of perceptual task. It is relevant to interpreting the drop in self-rating and the confidence covariate analyses.
- **Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015), "Algorithm aversion: People erroneously avoid algorithms after seeing them err," *JEP:General* 144, 114–126.** https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033
  Shows that how people weight algorithmic advice depends on observed algorithm behaviour. This is relevant to the competence-inference alternative explanation of Experiment 2B.
- **Sanfey, Rilling, Aronson, Nystrom & Cohen (2003), "The neural basis of economic decision-making in the Ultimatum Game," *Science* 300, 1755–1758.** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1082976
  The classic human-versus-computer partner control, in which identical behaviour from a computer elicits weaker social responses. It provides a precedent and a framework for interpreting Experiment 2B.

## Overall assessment

This is a clever, well-motivated paradigm with a genuinely interesting result. People appear to weight a partner's opinion by how much that partner weights theirs, even when the partner's accuracy is held fixed. The interaction in Experiment 2A/B is suggestive.

However:
- The theoretical claim (social signalling to minds) currently rests on a human-versus-computer contrast that also changes perceived competence, and possibly on non-randomized groups.
- The informational alternatives are handled with underpowered covariate models.
- There are several statistical and reporting errors.
- The paper does not use the trial-level variation that would make the reciprocity claim much more convincing.

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The required work is mostly re-analysis and clarification, not new data collection: trial-level reciprocity models, the self-rating dissociation argument, corrected statistics, and clarity on assignment and parameters. If the trial-level analysis holds, the paper would be a strong fit for Nature Communications.
