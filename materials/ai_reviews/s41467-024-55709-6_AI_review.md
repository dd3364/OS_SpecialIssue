# Review: "A Transparency Statement Improves Community-Police Interactions"

## Summary

The paper asks why community-policing contacts often fail to build trust. It proposes that civilians read an officer-initiated contact as a possible investigatory stop, and that a short opening statement of benign purpose ("I'm walking around trying to get to know the community") breaks that default reading. The evidence comes from four sources:

- **Qualitative fieldwork:** more than 500 hours with a community-policing unit and a patrol unit, used to generate the hypothesis.
- **Experiment 1:** a pre-registered field experiment (N = 232) in which seven on-duty officers approached civilians wearing wrist EDA sensors. Outcomes were self-reports, LIWC analyses of transcripts and written reflections, and skin conductance.
- **Experiment 2:** a pre-registered video experiment on Prolific (N = 969) that tests moderation by demographic group.
- **Experiments 3–7:** five vignette experiments that vary the content of the statement (enforcement purpose, vague friendliness) and the role of the speaker (grocery worker, park ranger).

The core contribution is a cheap, specific behavioural script that turns abstract procedural-justice advice into one sentence, with causal evidence from a live field setting.

## Strengths

- **Live field test with real officers.** Experiment 1 uses on-duty, armed, uniformed officers approaching civilians who did not expect them. That is rare and valuable. Most work on procedural-justice scripts uses vignettes or videos, or works at the level of whole departments.
- **The vignette series is well built.** Experiment 4 (stated enforcement purpose) and Experiment 5 (vague friendliness) separate "a stated reason" from "a benign reason" and from "being friendly". Experiments 6 and 7 (grocery worker, park ranger) give a dose-response logic on the power to arrest or harm. Together they make a coherent case for an attributional mechanism.
- **Good research practice.** Studies are pre-registered, data and code are posted, an internal meta-analysis is reported (Fig. 4), and the authors say plainly which analyses are confirmatory and which are exploratory.
- **Usable for practice.** The intervention takes about five minutes to train. That gives a clear route to scale-up trials.

## Key claims

### 1. The primary threat measure overlaps with the manipulation itself, which inflates the headline effect

**The problem.** Two of the three items in the pre-registered threat measure ask whether the officer "just wanted to get to know you" and "just wanted to make friendly conversation" (reverse-scored). The treatment is an officer saying they are "trying to get to know the community". A civilian who simply recalls the opening line will score lower on "threat" without feeling any less threatened. The scale is therefore close to a second manipulation check, which the authors themselves frame as "the inverse of trust."

The data fit this reading. In Experiment 2 the effect on the 3-item subset (−0.42 SD) is roughly 50% larger than on the full 10-item scale (−0.28 SD). The 3-item composite is also the outcome pooled in the Fig. 4 meta-analysis.

**Why it matters.** The paper's central causal claim is that transparency reduces threat. The most-used measure is the one least able to tell threat apart from accurate recall of the stated intent.

**What would address it:**
- Re-estimate every study on items that do not paraphrase the statement. In Experiment 1 that is "did not trust you". In Experiments 2–7 it is the seven added items, such as "going to accuse you of something", plus any fear or anxiety items.
- Report these estimates alongside the 3-item results in the main text and in Fig. 4.
- Experiment 1's PANAS threat-emotion result (38% vs. 19%) is the cleanest field evidence of reduced threat. Give it more weight, after fixing the inconsistent item list (see Specific comments).
- If the effect holds on the non-overlapping items, the paper becomes much stronger. If it shrinks, the abstract should be recalibrated.

### 2. Experiment 1's design does not isolate the statement from everything else officers did differently, and inference does not reflect shift-level assignment

**Officer behaviour is a confound.** Officers knew their condition and delivered the treatment. They were told to keep talking "in their own natural conversation style" afterwards. The treatment is therefore "transparency statement plus whatever officers did differently once they had framed the contact as relationship-building." The finding that civilians said about 175 more words in the treatment condition, in exchanges meant to last 1–2 minutes, suggests treatment conversations were longer or ran differently on the officer's side too.

**Inference is too optimistic.** Assignment was by shift, and only seven officers took part. The effective number of independent units is the number of shifts, not 232. The supplementary mixed model that clusters by shift gives p = .037 for threat and p = .025 for trust, so the result depends on how clustering is handled. The posterior for the primary outcome is only pr(ATE < 0) = .92, and the main-text intervals are 10th–90th percentiles. Together these make the evidence look firmer than it is.

The condition sizes also differ: 100 vs. 132 in Methods, 102 vs. 130 in Fig. 4. This needs an explanation in a shift-level design.

**What would address it:**
- Report the number of shifts per condition and the number of shifts and participants per officer.
- Report officer-by-condition cell sizes and officer-specific effects. Is the effect carried by one or two officers?
- Use randomization inference at the shift level, or a wild-cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2008), for the primary outcomes.
- Report 95% intervals in the main text.
- Code the officers' side of each transcript: duration, officer word count, number of questions, interrogative "what are you doing here"-type questions, and LIWC tone. Show which of these differ by condition. Then test whether the civilian outcomes survive adjustment, or treat officer behaviour as a mediator. This is achievable with existing data, and it is the only way to support the claim that the opening sentence itself is what does the work.

### 3. The framing of "transparency" overstates novelty relative to procedural justice and the authors' own "wise intervention" tradition

**The active ingredient is not transparency as such.** Experiment 4 shows that a fully transparent statement of enforcement purpose increases threat. Transparency per se is therefore not the active ingredient. The active ingredient is a credible signal of benign, non-enforcement intent that resolves attributional ambiguity.

**This idea is already a component of procedural justice.** The "trustworthy motives" component is exactly this. In the Queensland Community Engagement Trial, which the paper cites (ref. 21), the procedural-justice script had officers tell drivers they were stopped at random and explain their concern about drink-driving. Weisburd et al. (2022) trained officers in "evidencing trustworthy motives" and reduced arrests and civilian perceptions of harassment.

The introduction says procedural justice "has not yet made community policing more reliably effective" and presents the transparency statement as a separate method. A more accurate and stronger framing would be:

- The transparency statement is a minimal, standardized operationalization of the trustworthy-motives element, delivered at the moment of greatest ambiguity.
- The contribution is specificity, timing, and evidence on mechanism, not a new construct.

**The closest conceptual precedent is not cited.** That precedent is "wise feedback" (Yeager et al. 2014): an authority explicitly states benign intent to defuse mistrust across a racial divide. Positioning the work as a wise intervention for policing would sharpen the theory. It would also give a principled prediction for the moderation analyses: larger benefits for groups with more prior mistrust.

**The framing creates an ethical question the paper should address.** What if the officer's purpose is partly investigatory? Community contacts are also a source of intelligence. The paper defines the statement as "truthful", but it never discusses what happens to trust when such statements are later perceived as pretextual. Add a paragraph on this.

### 4. "Equally effective" for historically over-policed groups is not supported by non-significant moderation

**The problem.** The abstract says the statements "were equally effective for members of historically-overpoliced groups." The basis is no consistent moderation in Experiment 2, plus a few unspecified cases where minority groups benefited more (Table S7). Absence of evidence for moderation is not evidence of equivalence. The subgroups are small: about 12% Black (n ≈ 116) and 4% Latino (n ≈ 39) in Experiment 2, and about 21 Black participants in Experiment 1.

The Experiment 2 sample is also described as "representative of the U.S. population in terms of … race/ethnicity", but only 4% are Latino. That is far below the U.S. share.

**What would address it:**
- Report subgroup-specific ATEs with intervals for each over-policed group.
- Run equivalence tests (Lakens 2017) against a stated smallest effect of interest, and pool across studies where possible.
- Correct for the number of moderators examined.
- Rephrase the claim as "we found no evidence of smaller effects, though precision for some groups was limited."
- Correct the representativeness description.

### 5. The physiological evidence is too fragile to count as corroboration

**Interpretation problems:**
- The BPS model separates challenge from threat with cardiovascular indices: cardiac output and total peripheral resistance (Seery 2011). EDA rises under both states.
- Reading higher initial EDA as "positive openness" and later decline as "recovery" is theoretically possible. But the opposite pattern could have been read just as easily as threat, so the test is not falsifiable as framed.

**Measurement problems:**
- The E4 samples EDA at 4 Hz. A second derivative over 10-second windows of a wrist signal is very noisy.
- Wrist EDA correlated poorly with finger EDA in validation work (Menghini et al. 2019).
- The processing pipeline describes 5-minute windows with 4-minute overlap and "per-minute" resolution. That cannot be reconciled with 10-second epochs in a 1–2 minute interaction. Please clarify exactly what was analysed.
- Physiological data exist for 177 of 232 participants, because participants had to press a button when someone began talking to them. Pressing could plausibly differ by condition.

**What would address it:**
- Report missingness by condition.
- Report conventional metrics (tonic SCL level, SCR counts) alongside the second derivative.
- Show robustness to the choice of window.
- Frame the EDA results in the main text as exploratory and consistent with, rather than corroborating, the self-report results. Alternatively, move them to the SI.
- Drop "corroboration" from the abstract unless the above holds up.

## Specific comments

- **Trust measure.** Results describe 2 items; Methods describe 3 adapted items (α = .63). Experiment 2 switches to the integrity subscale and Experiment 3 to benevolence. State which version is used where. The low α in Experiment 1 deserves comment.
- **Threat emotions.** Results list "afraid, nervous, upset, hostile, ashamed". Methods add "alert", which is not plausibly a threat emotion. Report the result with and without "alert". "ATE = −.15pp" should read −15 percentage points.
- **Typos in effect sizes.** "ATE = 46 SD" should be .46 SD. In Experiment 7, "pr(ATE > 0) > .99" should be pr(ATE < 0).
- **LIWC analysis.** Authenticity is reported for the final third of civilian speech, but four summary dimensions × three segments × two speakers were available. Report all of them (for example, in the SI) to show that the authenticity result is not selected. Authenticity scores are also sensitive to text length, and treatment transcripts were longer. Adjust for word count.
- **Experiment 2 control script.** The Methods text says the control officer said "Hi, how are you doing?", but the script shown is "I'm just out here saying hi and seeing how things are going." Clarify which was used. If it was the latter, note that this control closely resembles the "ambiguously positive" condition of Experiment 5. That makes Experiment 2 a more demanding test, which strengthens the paper.
- **Experiment 4 labels.** The condition is called "aggressive policing" in text, "Ambivalent Transparency" in the script, and "ATE_ambiguous" in the contrast. The script line also repeats "Hi, how are you doing?". Use one label throughout.
- **Vignette wording, Experiments 3, 4, 6 and 7.** The treatment says "My boss is asking me to talk to people." This attributes the motive to a supervisor, unlike the officer-owned statements in Experiments 1, 2 and 5. Note this difference, and say whether effects differ by statement wording across studies.
- **Experiment 1 sample.** Participants were recruited partly through university email, seated around a university building, and had a mean age of 22. Describing them as residents of "a community known for recent accusations of officers' mistreatment" overstates how typical they are. Characterize the sample candidly and discuss what it means for external validity.
- **Box 1.** The community-policing example ("I know what you're doing … you shouldn't be doing that here") is a warning, not a statement of benevolent purpose. Use a clearer exemplar. Also note that the two units differ in officer selection, not only in tactics. Officers in the pilot unit were chosen for their relationship skills.
- **Reference errors.** Several citations do not match their claims. Please audit the whole list.
  - PANAS-SF is cited to Callanan et al. (2020, museum parent–child interaction; ref. 52). It should be Thompson (2007).
  - The metafor meta-analysis in Fig. 4 is cited to ref. 25 (Bayesian causal forests). It should be Viechtbauer (2010).
  - "TSST" in the PPG paragraph cites ref. 39 (Voigt et al.) instead of ref. 55.
  - "TPR" cites ref. 30 (an idiographic executive-function study).
  - The claim that trust is at an all-time low cites MacDonald (2002).
  - Ref. 25 should cite the published version of the BCF paper (Hahn, Murray & Carvalho 2020).
- **Fig. 4.** For Experiment 3, explain why n = 305 when N = 609 (only half of participants answered threat items). Consider adding a panel with the non-overlapping threat items (see Key claim 1).
- **Discussion.** The "twice as many felt inspired" result comes from one exploratory PANAS item. It should not be a headline finding in the Discussion.
- **Title and abstract.** The outcomes are immediate perceptions after a single 1–2 minute contact. "Improves community-police interactions" is acceptable, but the abstract should say these are initial-encounter perceptions. It should also state that no downstream behaviour such as cooperation or information-sharing was measured.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Mazerolle, Antrobus, Bennett & Tyler (2013), "Shaping citizen perceptions of police legitimacy: a randomized field trial of procedural justice," *Criminology* 51, 33–63.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-9125.2012.00289.x
  The QCET script included telling drivers why they were stopped and conveying trustworthy motives. It is the most direct field precedent for a "reason-giving" opening, and the novelty framing must engage it. The companion report, ref. 21, is at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11292-012-9160-1.
- **Weisburd et al. (2022), "Reforming the police through procedural justice training: A multicity randomized trial at crime hot spots," *PNAS* 119, e2118780119.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2118780119
  A randomized trial in which training in "evidencing trustworthy motives" changed officer behaviour and civilian perceptions of harassment. It contradicts the claim that procedural justice has not produced reliable effects, and offers a model for the longitudinal follow-up the authors propose.
- **Yeager et al. (2014), "Breaking the cycle of mistrust: Wise interventions to provide critical feedback across the racial divide," *J. Exp. Psychol. Gen.* 143, 804–824.** https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033906
  The closest conceptual analogue: an authority states benign intent explicitly to resolve attributional ambiguity, with larger effects among more mistrustful groups. It grounds both the mechanism and the moderation predictions.
- **Lowrey, Maguire & Bennett (2016), "Testing the effects of procedural justice and overaccommodation in traffic stops: a randomized experiment," *Criminal Justice and Behavior* 43, 1430–1449.** https://doi.org/10.1177/0093854816639330
  A video experiment separating procedural justice from mere friendliness (overaccommodation). It parallels the logic of Experiment 5, and its encounter-specific versus general-attitude distinction matches this paper's outcomes.
- **Owens, Weisburd, Amendola & Alpert (2018), "Can you build a better cop?" *Criminology & Public Policy* 17, 41–87.** https://doi.org/10.1111/1745-9133.12337
  A randomized evaluation of procedural-justice supervision in Seattle. It is relevant to the claim that abstract procedural-justice training leaves too much to officer discretion.
- **Camp, Voigt, Jurafsky & Eberhardt (2021), "The thin blue waveform: Racial disparities in officer prosody undermine institutional trust in the police," *JPSP* 121, 1157–1171.** https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000270
  Shows that officers' delivery, not only their words, shapes trust. It supports coding officer tone and prosody in the Experiment 1 audio as a check on the confound in Key claim 2.
- **Seery (2011), "Challenge or threat? Cardiovascular indexes of resilience and vulnerability to potential stress in humans," *Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev.* 35, 1603–1610.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2011.03.003
  Establishes that challenge and threat are indexed by cardiac output and TPR, not EDA. It bears directly on how the SCL results are interpreted.
- **Menghini et al. (2019), "Stressing the accuracy: Wrist-worn wearable sensor validation over different conditions," *Psychophysiology* 56, e13441.** https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13441
  An E4 validation finding that wrist skin conductance did not track finger EDA. It should temper the EDA claims.
- **Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008), "Bootstrap-based improvements for inference with clustered errors," *Rev. Econ. Stat.* 90, 414–427.** https://doi.org/10.1162/rest.90.3.414
  Inference with few clusters, appropriate for shift-level assignment with seven officers.
- **Lakens (2017), "Equivalence tests: A practical primer for t tests, correlations, and meta-analyses," *SPPS* 8, 355–362.** https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550617697177
  The tool needed to support, or retract, the "equally effective" claim.
- **Thompson (2007), I-PANAS-SF, *J. Cross-Cult. Psychol.* 38, 227–242.** https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022106297301
  The correct source for the short-form PANAS.
- **Viechtbauer (2010), metafor, *J. Stat. Softw.* 36(3).** https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v036.i03
  The correct citation for the Fig. 4 meta-analysis.

## Overall assessment

The paper is promising and the question matters. A live field experiment with on-duty officers, plus a mechanism-dissecting vignette series, is a rare and valuable combination, and the practical message is clear.

The central claims currently outrun the evidence, for three reasons:
- The primary threat scale largely restates the manipulation.
- The field experiment cannot separate the opening sentence from officers' subsequent behaviour, and its clustered inference is only marginal.
- The "equally effective" and "physiological corroboration" claims rest on null moderation tests and a fragile EDA metric.

Each problem can be addressed with re-analyses of existing data and more careful framing. That framing should position the work as a precise operationalization of procedural justice's trustworthy-motives component, and as a wise intervention.

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The core finding may well survive. But re-estimating on non-overlapping outcome items, adding shift-level inference, analysing officer behaviour, and reframing novelty are needed before the paper's claims are secure.
