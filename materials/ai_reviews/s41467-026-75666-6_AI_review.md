# Review: "High costs of capital hinder the equity, speed and abatement potential of solar and wind deployment globally"

## Summary

The paper builds country- and technology-specific nominal WACC estimates for 2024 for 176 countries. It adapts IRENA's build-up approach: risk-free rate plus Damodaran country risk premium or default spread, a technology premium tied to market maturity, a 2% lender margin, and a debt share that falls linearly with country risk from 80% to 40%. These estimates are combined with MERRA-2-based solar and wind profiles, GlobCover land-availability factors and IRENA regional capex. The result is grid-cell LCOEs, regional LCOE-supply curves and lifetime abatement curves for solar PV, onshore and offshore wind. These are compared under a "National" and a "Uniform" scenario, the latter using the Annex II average WACC of 8.2/8.1/9.0%. The headline findings are:
- National WACCs raise LCOE in developing countries by about US$19 and 28/MWh for solar and onshore wind.
- The financing share of LCOE rises from about 40% to up to 78%.
- Large shares of African and Asian potential drop out of Lazard's benchmark range.
- Lifetime abatement per MW is up to 20× higher in high-carbon-intensity developing countries.

The contribution is an updated, spatially explicit, multi-technology global picture of how financing costs reshape the cost and abatement geography of solar and wind.

## Strengths

- **The question matters for policy and the framing is timely.** It links the COP28 tripling goal, the NCQG and concessional-finance debates to a quantitative, spatial picture.
- **The integration is genuine.** The paper combines resource, land-use, cost-of-capital and grid-intensity layers at about 96,000 grid points for three technologies, which is more than most predecessors. In particular, Ondraczek et al. (2015) covered solar only, at country level.
- **The WACC method improves on IRENA's in one respect.** The debt share varies continuously with country risk rather than in three maturity steps, and the components (cost of debt/equity, technology premium, tax) are laid out clearly.
- **The regional supply curves (Fig. 5) are an effective format.** Showing National and Uniform curves together conveys the magnitude of the financing penalty at a glance.
- **Code is public (GitHub)** and a supplementary dataset is provided, which will make the WACC estimates reusable.

## Key claims

### 1. The WACC estimates, on which every result rests, are neither validated against observed financing costs nor given uncertainty ranges (load-bearing)

All headline numbers come from the difference between modelled national WACCs and an 8.2% Annex II average. Yet the WACC model is never compared with observed project-level data. This matters because the build-up approach has well-known biases:
- **Country risk premiums are added in full.** Damodaran's CRPs are sovereign-equity premia. Renewable projects in many emerging and developing economies (EMDEs) are financed in hard currency, under PPAs with DFI guarantees, political-risk insurance and blended finance, and so do not pay the full sovereign premium.
- **Upper-end values look implausible.** Estimates of up to about 30–32% nominal in countries in crisis are hard to reconcile with financed projects.

Outside evidence suggests a smaller gap than "up to triple" for the major EMDE markets:
- **IEA Cost of Capital Observatory** (which the authors cite as ref. 80): first-round surveys reported median solar WACCs of just under 10% in India and Mexico and about 13% in Brazil.
- **Auction prices:** Dobrotkova et al. (2018) document competitive solar prices of US$60–80/MWh in many developing countries.
- **Auction-implied WACC:** Egli et al. (2023, ref. 55) give a method to back out WACC from auction results.

**What would address it:**
- **Validate the estimates.** Scatter the modelled WACC against (i) the IEA Observatory values, (ii) auction-implied WACCs (Egli et al. 2023) and (iii) Steffen (2020)'s compiled evidence, and report the bias and RMSE.
- **Propagate parameter uncertainty.** Vary the debt-share bounds, the lender margin, technology premia, and full versus partial CRP pass-through, and report ranges on the US$/MWh, financing-share and US$bn headline numbers.
- **Separate the extreme cases.** Flag countries where no commercial project finance is observed, and report results with and without them. The paper states that crisis countries (Ukraine, Venezuela, South Sudan) drive the largest increases.

### 2. Several headline numbers are inconsistent, and the US$574/888bn metric is not interpretable as stated (load-bearing for the abstract)

- **The average LCOE increase is reported three different ways.**
  - Abstract and Results: US$19 and 28/MWh for solar and onshore wind (28% and 35%), and US$31/MWh for offshore.
  - Introduction: US$15.4 and 37.8/MWh "average worldwide".
  - Discussion: US$15, 38 and 29/MWh.

  These appear to mix "developing-country average", "global grid-cell average" and possibly different weightings. Define one averaging basis (capacity-, potential- or area-weighted; which country set) and use it everywhere.
- **The financing share is reported as "40–42% up to 78%"** (Abstract, Results) and as "40% to nearly 76%" (Introduction).
- **The technology order of US$574bn/US$888bn is ambiguous.** The abstract lists them in solar-then-wind order. The Results attribute them to "onshore wind and solar PV, respectively".
- **The US$574/888bn scenario is far beyond total electricity demand.** It assumes 5% of technical potential is developed. 5% of 830,000 TWh (solar) is about 41,500 TWh, and 5% of 935,000 TWh (onshore) is about 46,750 TWh. Each exceeds 2023 global generation, which the paper itself puts near 30,000 TWh (Africa's 165,000 TWh = "5.4 times"). The figures also do not obviously equal the per-MWh increases multiplied by those volumes.

  **Fix:** tie the annual-cost metric to a policy-relevant deployment volume, for example the COP28 tripling trajectory allocated by region or least-cost siting to meet regional demand growth. Report it as an annual financing premium, which is far more useful for the concessional-finance argument in the Discussion.

### 3. The comparisons in the "cost-competitive" and "abatement" analyses need sharper counterfactuals

- **Lazard is a weak global yardstick.** Lazard's unsubsidized US LCOE ranges are used as the global viability benchmark (Africa's sub-US$55/MWh solar share falling from 89.7% to 23.5%, and so on). They embed US costs and US financing assumptions. Viability in Africa or Central Asia depends on the local alternative: new-build gas or coal LCOE, diesel, import parity, or tariffs. Use a country-specific counterfactual, even a simple fossil LCOE using the same national WACC. Fossil plants also face the higher national WACC, which matters for the competitiveness claim and is currently ignored (cf. Hirth & Steckel 2016).
- **Average versus marginal emission factors.** Abatement assumes each MWh displaces the grid-average intensity, and that this intensity falls linearly to zero by 2045 in every country. The "20×" contrast with France, Norway and the UK partly reflects near-zero averages in those systems. A combined-margin factor, such as the IFI harmonised default grid factors for intermittent renewables, is the standard tool used by MDBs. It would make the abatement numbers usable by the climate-finance audience the paper addresses.
- **Uniform decline path.** A 2045 linear decline everywhere, admitted in Methods to overstate developed-country abatement, should be replaced by, or bounded with, region-specific pathways.
- **A sharper paper is hiding here.** Combine ΔLCOE and abatement into a "financing-cost penalty per tonne abated" (US$/tCO₂) by grid cell or country. That directly answers the Discussion's question of where concessional finance buys the most abatement per dollar, and it would distinguish this work from Ondraczek et al. (2015), IRENA (2023) and Calcaterra et al. (2024), which already establish that WACC raises LCOE in developing countries.

### 4. Some "findings" are built into the assumptions, and novelty needs to be positioned precisely

- **The GDP link is mechanical.** "The cost of capital is inversely related to economic development" (Abstract, Discussion) follows mechanically from Damodaran's CRPs, which are derived from sovereign ratings and CDS spreads. Those are strongly correlated with income. Present it as an input property, or test it with observed WACCs (Key claim 1).
- **The financing-share result is partly arithmetic.** The near-doubling of the financing share follows from LCOE algebra once WACC rises from about 8% to 15–30%. It is not an independent result.
- **Earlier work covers the core claim.** Country-specific WACC already appears in Ondraczek et al. (2015, a 143-country solar LCOE map), Schyska & Kies (2020), Sweerts et al. (2019), Agutu et al. (2022) and Calcaterra et al. (2024). The Introduction should state exactly what is new: multi-technology grid-cell supply curves, offshore wind, an abatement layer, and 2024 parameters.

## Specific comments

- **Results, Fig. 3 text.** The cross-reference reads "Error! Reference source not found."
- **Fig. 4 caption.** It says "Country scenario"; use "National" consistently.
- **Results, para. after Fig. 2.** "Costs of capital that are at least double those in developing countries" should read *developed*.
- **Discussion, Bangladesh example.** "Nearly double that in developing countries (14%)" should read *developed*. Also, a 14% WACC is not "nearly double" 8.2%; check the number.
- **Abstract vs. Methods country count.** The abstract says "over 150 countries" and Methods says 176. Reconcile.
- **Equations.** The text refers to Equation [5] for WACC, but the WACC equation is [2] and there is no [5]. Check that Eqs. [1]–[4] and [6]–[7] render correctly, including the tilt-angle formula and the tax term τ.
- **Nominal versus real consistency.** The WACCs are nominal (USD), but IRENA capex/opex and the Lazard/IRENA LCOE benchmarks are not stated on the same basis. State whether LCOE is a nominal or real levelised value, and convert WACC (or deflate cash flows) consistently. A nominal WACC applied to constant-dollar cash flows overstates LCOE, and it does so most in high-WACC countries.
- **Pre-tax versus after-tax.** Eq. [2] is an after-tax WACC, but the LCOE formula has no tax or depreciation terms. Either use a pre-tax WACC or model taxes explicitly.
- **Economic lifetime.** 20 years is short for current solar and onshore wind assets. Because the financing share depends strongly on lifetime, add a 25–30-year sensitivity.
- **Financing-share definition (Fig. 4).** "Subtracting the contributions of capital costs and operating costs" needs a formula. Is the reference LCOE the one at a 0% discount rate?
- **Offshore potential.** Methods assign 100% availability offshore, with no stated water-depth, distance-to-shore, EEZ or shipping/protected-area exclusions. The resulting 15,000 TWh is far below the IEA's (2019) estimates of about 420,000 TWh total and about 36,000 TWh for near-shore, shallow (<60 m) sites, which implies unstated restrictions, probably from MERRA-2 coastal cells. Document the offshore domain explicitly.
- **Capex assignment.** Solar capex data exist for only 30 markets, and a global average of US$876/kW fills gaps. Report how much of the African and Central Asian potential relies on imputed capex. This interacts with the WACC effect, since high-risk countries also tend to have higher capex.
- **Weather year.** A single MERRA-2 year is used; state which. Note interannual variability, and the known MERRA-2 wind bias that Renewables.ninja corrects.
- **Abatement Methods.** Two caps are used: 10% of technical potential per cell, and national 2023 generation. Explain how they interact, and why national generation (rather than projected 2030/2040 demand) is the relevant cap.
- **Data availability.** "Available upon request" is unlikely to satisfy Nature-family policy. Deposit the grid-cell LCOE, potential and abatement outputs and the WACC table in a public repository with a DOI.
- **Figures 2/ED 3.** The two are near-duplicates. Consider moving one to the Supplement and using the main-text slot for the validation scatter from Key claim 1.

## Literature and data to engage

- **IEA Cost of Capital Observatory (ref. 80, already cited but unused for validation).** https://www.iea.org/reports/cost-of-capital-observatory/dashboard-2. Observed, survey-based WACC for solar, wind and gas in major EMDEs; the natural benchmark for Key claim 1. A secondary summary of the first survey round reports medians of about 10% (India, Mexico) and 13% (Brazil): https://payneinstitute.mines.edu/cost-of-capital-observatory-launch-shows-there-is-much-work-to-do/.
- **Dobrotkova, Z., Surana, K. & Audinet, P. (2018). The price of solar energy: Comparing competitive auctions for utility-scale solar PV in developing countries. Energy Policy 118, 133–148.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2018.03.036. Observed solar prices in developing countries, a direct check on the modelled National-scenario LCOEs.
- **Ondraczek, J., Komendantova, N. & Patt, A. (2015). WACC the dog. Renewable Energy 75, 888–898.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2014.10.053. Already cited, but only for a side point. It is the direct predecessor (143-country solar LCOE with national WACC) and must be engaged explicitly in the novelty statement.
- **Steffen, B. (2020). Estimating the cost of capital for renewable energy projects. Energy Economics 88, 104783.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eneco.2020.104783. Already cited. Use its 46-country empirical compilation for validation, not only as background.
- **Calcaterra, M. et al. (2024). Reducing the cost of capital to finance the energy transition in developing countries. Nature Energy 9, 1241–1251.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-024-01606-7. Already cited. It quantifies the financing burden and risk-premium convergence in five IAMs. The paper should state how its grid-cell results add to that.
- **Hirth, L. & Steckel, J. C. (2016). The role of capital costs in decarbonizing the electricity sector. Environmental Research Letters 11, 114010.** https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/11/11/114010. Shows that high WACC also changes the renewables-versus-fossil comparison, because capital intensity differs. This is relevant to the missing fossil counterfactual in Key claim 3.
- **Schyska, B. U. & Kies, A. (2020). How regional differences in cost of capital influence the optimal design of power systems. Applied Energy 262, 114523.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2020.114523. Shows that heterogeneous WACC shifts the wind/solar balance, which bears on the paper's technology comparison.
- **IFI Technical Working Group. Harmonized IFI default grid emission factors (combined margin).** https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/Harmonized_Grid_Emission_factor_data_set.pdf. A standard, country-level marginal-type emission factor set for intermittent renewables. Use it instead of, or alongside, Ember grid averages for the abatement analysis.
- **IEA (2019). Offshore Wind Outlook 2019.** https://www.iea.org/reports/offshore-wind-outlook-2019. A global offshore technical-potential benchmark, with depth and distance breakdowns, against which to reconcile the 15,000 TWh estimate.

## Overall assessment

This is a useful, well-integrated global dataset on an important policy question. But in its current form the headline numbers are internally inconsistent, and they rest on an unvalidated WACC model with no uncertainty ranges. The US$bn cost metric uses a deployment volume larger than global electricity demand, and the competitiveness and abatement comparisons use a US benchmark and grid-average factors. Validating the WACCs against the IEA Observatory and auction data, reconciling the numbers, and recasting the abatement layer as a financing-cost-per-tonne metric would turn a descriptive update into a distinctive contribution suited to *Nature Communications* or *Nature Energy*. **Recommendation: major revisions.**
