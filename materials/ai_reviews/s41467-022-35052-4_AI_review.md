# Review: "Surfacing norms to increase vaccine acceptance"

## Summary

The paper asks whether telling people how many others in their country say they will take a COVID-19 vaccine (a descriptive norm) raises their own stated intention to vaccinate. Theory gives no clear sign: conformity and social learning push intentions up, while free-riding pushes them down. The authors embedded a pre-registered experiment in a Facebook-recruited survey that ran in 23 countries from October 2020 to March 2021 (analytic N = 484,239; 365,593 in the main vaccine comparison). They randomized whether the normative message came before or after the outcome question, which behaviour it described (vaccines, masks or distancing), and whether it used a narrow ("Yes") or broad ("Yes" + "Don't know") definition of acceptance. On a five-point scale, the broad and narrow messages raise intentions by 0.039 and 0.033. The effects are concentrated among baseline "Don't know" respondents, and the point estimate is positive in 22 of 23 countries. The messages also raise beliefs about how many others will vaccinate. The main contributions are a very large, multi-country causal estimate and the policy message that truthful norm information is at worst harmless and on average helpful.

## Strengths

- **Scale and design.** The experiment has hundreds of thousands of respondents in 23 countries, a pre-registered plan, and two kinds of comparison: information before vs. after the outcome, and vaccine vs. other-behaviour information. The design makes good use of a survey that already existed.
- **Effects are measured across the whole scale.** The authors report effects at each threshold of the outcome scale (Tables S8–S10, S14), not just a mean shift. This shows where on the scale people move (from Unsure to Probably, and from Probably to Definitely).
- **Manipulation check.** The paper shows that the messages actually changed beliefs about others (Fig. S5, Table S6). This is essential for a norms intervention and is often missing.
- **Transparency about limits.** Post hoc analyses are labelled as such. The paper reports the null difference between the under and above belief groups (p = 0.38/0.31) and that the effect shrinks when treatment and outcome are further apart (p = 0.02/0.03).
- **Practical value.** A message that uses only truthful aggregate information and can be scaled up is directly useful to public health communicators.

## Key claims

**1. Differential attrition and the choice of analytic sample need a principled treatment, not only a switch of comparison group.** The intent-to-treat randomization test rejects equal assignment (Table S1, p = 0.011). Conditioning on completion and on receiving a full weight drops about 40% of respondents. In the final sample, some pre-treatment covariates are imbalanced (Table S4: know a positive case p = 0.029, infection severity p = 0.011, distancing and mask norms p = 0.028/0.041). The fallback comparison group, respondents who saw mask or distancing information, has its own problem. Baseline vaccine acceptance differs sharply between people shown vaccine information and people shown mask information (Table S5, VM p < 0.001; 1.524 vs 1.505). That is the key prognostic variable, and the imbalance persists even with period controls. Fig. S5b also shows that other-behaviour information shifts vaccine-norm beliefs, so this group is not a clean placebo.
*Fix:*
- Report outcome-question completion rates by arm, using the ITT dataset.
- Report Lee (2009) trimming bounds for the main effects.
- Explain why baseline vaccine acceptance is imbalanced across behaviour arms. If the cause is wave-level shifts in assignment probability, show results with wave × country fixed effects and inverse-probability weights for the changing assignment shares.
- Put the attrition diagnostics in the main text. A ~40% exclusion rate will be the first question any referee asks.

**2. The framing outruns the effect size and the evidence on mechanism.** The abstract and discussion call the effects "substantial", but the absolute effects are small: 1.6 percentage points more people at "at least Probably" (broad message), and 0.039 on a five-point scale, which is about 0.03 SD of the raw outcome (Table S7 gives a residual SE of 0.81 and R² of 0.61, implying a raw SD of about 1.3). The 4.9% figure is a relative reduction in a minority category. Saying the effect is "over a third of" the November–January secular change compares an immediate post-message survey response with a population-level change over time, so the two are not like for like.
The mechanism claim, that "the treatment works through revising beliefs about descriptive norms upwards", rests on subgroup patterns that are not statistically distinguishable (under vs above p = 0.38/0.31). The between-group prediction was not supported either (p = 0.87), and the treatment had no effect on mask or distancing intentions.
*Fix:*
- Report standardized effects and absolute changes in percentage points first.
- Describe the effect as "modest but highly consistent and cheap", which is a strong and defensible message.
- Run the pre-registered instrumental-variables analysis (Hypothesis 3) on the random subsample with post-treatment norm beliefs: randomized message as the instrument, belief as the endogenous variable. State the exclusion-restriction caveat, since the message also carries the "your responses help researchers" framing. This would turn a suggestive mechanism into an estimate of how much intentions change per point of belief, which is the quantity communicators need.
- Discuss Gelman & Stern's point, since the paper currently reads significant-vs-not-significant subgroup results as mechanism evidence.

**3. The dose-response and "accurate information" claims need tightening.** Fig. S11 and Fig. S10 infer that higher displayed numbers produce larger effects. But the displayed number was set by each country's acceptance level in each wave, not randomized, so countries with higher numbers also differ in baseline acceptance, in the share of fence-sitters, and in vaccine rollout timing. It is also unclear whether "accurate" means "accurate for the population". Table S15 implies that the survey overstates vaccination (slope 0.79 on the survey share). Independent work shows that a large Facebook-recruited US survey overestimated vaccine uptake by about 17 points even after weighting (Bradley et al., 2021). The numbers shown to respondents may therefore have been inflated estimates, which matters ethically and for interpreting the dose-response.
*Fix:*
- Estimate the dose-response using only within-country, across-wave variation in the displayed number, with country fixed effects and controls for baseline acceptance. Otherwise drop the causal language around Figs. S10–S11.
- Replace "accurate" with "survey-based" or "truthful to the survey estimate", and discuss the representativeness of the sample frame.
- Note that the narrow vs broad contrast is the only randomized variation in the number shown, and that it moved nothing detectably. This weakens the dose-response story and should be said plainly.

**4. The translation from intentions to uptake (23.1 and 17.2 percentage points) is not credible as stated.** These numbers apply observational differences in uptake between intention categories, taken from a US CloudResearch panel of 1,350 people, to people whose intentions were moved by a message in 23 countries. This assumes that an induced change in intention carries the same uptake rate as a naturally occurring one. Meta-analytic evidence shows that experimentally induced intention changes translate into behaviour at roughly half the size (Webb & Sheeran, 2006). Field trials of COVID-19 vaccination messaging show that real uptake effects depend heavily on access and salience (Dai et al., 2021).
*Fix:* Present these numbers as an upper bound under an explicit assumption, or drop them, and cite the intention–behaviour evidence.

## Specific comments

- **Novelty claim (Intro, "no other quantitative causal assessments").** Before publication, several experiments tested descriptive-norm messages for COVID-19 vaccination. Sinclair & Agerström (2021) found weak effects in a UK experiment. Agranov et al. (2021) estimated that norms and herding outweigh free-riding. Narrow the claim to "the first large-scale, multi-country field-survey experiment using truthful norm information", which is true and still strong. Reconciling your positive result with Sinclair & Agerström's weak one (sample, wording, truthful vs. stylized numbers, timing) would be a useful paragraph.
- **Theory (Discussion).** The policy conclusion recommends communicating "widespread and *growing* acceptance", but the treatment conveyed a static norm. Either cite the dynamic-norms literature (Sparkman & Walton, 2017) as a hypothesis for future work, or remove "growing" from the abstract.
- **Boomerang risk.** The theory section frames the risk of harm as free-riding. The classic risk with descriptive norms is instead that people above the norm move down towards it (Schultz et al., 2007). Your "above" group (Table S11: 0.023/0.026, not significant) is the right place to test this. Report it explicitly as a boomerang test, including the distributional effects for that group.
- **Researcher demand (Limitations).** The argument that demand effects should also show up for masks and distancing assumes those outcomes are equally responsive to demand. Ceiling effects on mask intentions (mean around 3.6 of 4) could explain the null result. Note this.
- **Fig. 3c / mixed model.** State whether the country estimates are partially pooled, and report the estimated between-country SD of treatment effects with its interval. "Most countries are statistically indistinguishable from the grand mean" is a weak test given the country-level sample sizes.
- **Equation rendering.** Equations [1], [2], (S3) and (S4) are garbled in the submitted text, with summation indices separated from their terms. Check the typesetting.
- **Fig. 2 colour coding.** The figure compares "community" beliefs with country-level norms. The text acknowledges the mismatch, but the colour bands invite reading people as "underestimators". Add a caveat in the caption.
- **Table S5.** The "knowledge existing treatments" means (0.159, 0.209, 0.555) differ greatly across arms despite non-significant p-values. Check the coding or the subsample used.
- **Code availability.** "Will be made public" should become a link before submission.

## Literature and data to engage

- Sinclair, S. & Agerström, J. (2021). Do social norms influence young people's willingness to take the COVID-19 vaccine? *Health Communication*. https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2021.1937832. A pre-registered norms-message experiment with weak effects; it directly qualifies the "no other causal assessments" claim and is a useful contrast.
- Agranov, M., Elliott, M. & Ortoleva, P. (2021). The importance of social norms against strategic effects: The case of Covid-19 vaccine uptake. *Economics Letters* 206, 109979. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v206y2021ics0165176521002561.html. Separately estimates herding, norms and free-riding for COVID-19 vaccination, which is exactly the trade-off your introduction poses.
- Bursztyn, L., González, A. L. & Yanagizawa-Drott, D. (2020). Misperceived social norms: Women working outside the home in Saudi Arabia. *American Economic Review* 110(10). https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180975. The canonical "correct misperceived norms" field experiment; it provides the theoretical framing and a template for linking belief correction to behaviour.
- Schultz, P. W. et al. (2007). The constructive, destructive, and reconstructive power of social norms. *Psychological Science* 18(5). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x. The boomerang effect for people above the norm, the right frame for your "above" subgroup.
- Sparkman, G. & Walton, G. M. (2017). Dynamic norms promote sustainable behavior, even if it is counternormative. *Psychological Science* 28. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617719950. Relevant to your "growing acceptance" recommendation and to countries with a low static norm.
- Graupensperger, S., Abdallah, D. A. & Lee, C. M. (2021). Social norms and vaccine uptake: College students' COVID vaccination intentions, attitudes, and estimated peer norms. *Vaccine* 39(15). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vaccine.2021.03.018. Observational norm–intention links and peer-norm misperception for COVID-19 vaccines; complements Fig. 1 inset and Fig. 2.
- Lee, D. S. (2009). Training, wages, and sample selection: Estimating sharp bounds on treatment effects. *Review of Economic Studies* 76(3). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-937X.2009.00536.x. The standard bounding approach for the differential attrition in Table S1.
- Webb, T. L. & Sheeran, P. (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? *Psychological Bulletin* 132(2). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249. Induced intention changes translate into behaviour at roughly half the size; needed to temper the 23.1/17.2 pp projections.
- Dai, H. et al. (2021). Behavioural nudges increase COVID-19 vaccinations. *Nature* 597. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03843-2. Field evidence on actual COVID-19 vaccine uptake; it also found that hypothetical online experiments diverged from field results, a caution relevant to intention outcomes.
- Bradley, V. C. et al. (2021). Unrepresentative big surveys significantly overestimate US vaccine uptake. arXiv:2106.05818. https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05818v1. Shows that a large, weighted Facebook-recruited survey overstated uptake; it bears on whether the numbers shown were "accurate".
- Gelman, A. & Stern, H. (2006). The difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself statistically significant. *The American Statistician* 60(4). https://doi.org/10.1198/000313006X152649. Relevant to the subgroup-based mechanism argument.

## Overall assessment

This is a valuable, well-powered, pre-registered experiment with a clear and useful finding: truthful descriptive-norm information modestly but consistently raises stated vaccine intentions across very different countries, with no sign of backfire. Its weaknesses are in framing and inference rather than design. The attrition and imbalance diagnostics need a principled treatment. The effect is described as larger, and the mechanism and dose-response as better established, than the evidence supports. The projection from intentions to uptake is not defensible as written. All of this can be fixed with the existing data (bounds, within-country dose-response, the pre-registered IV analysis) and more careful language. **Recommendation: minor revisions.** I would bring forward the attrition analysis and the IV mediation estimate before submitting to a general-interest journal such as *Nature Communications* or *Nature Human Behaviour*.
