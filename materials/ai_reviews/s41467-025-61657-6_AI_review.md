# Review: "Targeting climate finance for global forests"

## Summary

The paper uses the Global Timber Model (GTM) to estimate what it would cost to meet forest-based mitigation targets in 2030. The targets are LULUCF goals from the 2015–2021 NDCs of 44 countries, aggregated into 16 GTM regions. Each region's target is matched against GTM projections under eight carbon-price paths ($5–$200/tCO2 in the starting year, rising 3%/yr), and the authors report the price and finance each region needs. They then compare this with a "Global Coordination" scenario: one uniform $20/tCO2 price with roughly the same total spending. Headline results: meeting all NDCs needs $28–70 bn/yr in 2030. Several regions (US, Canada, Russia, China, Oceania, rest of South America) reach their targets with no finance at all. A uniform $20 price buys about 4.6 GtCO2 against 1.6 GtCO2 for the upper-bound NDCs, for about 8% more money. The core contribution is the first region-by-region mapping of forest-NDC ambition onto a dynamic forest-sector model with market feedbacks. The policy message is that current forest NDCs are spread inefficiently across regions.

## Strengths

- **A useful, policy-relevant question.** Turning heterogeneous LULUCF NDC targets into a regional finance-needs map, with an eye on the NCQG and the 2025 NDC round, is timely. Few studies link NDC targets to a forest-sector model that represents timber markets, land competition and management intensity.
- **A sensible harmonisation step.** The authors apply each country's GHGI-based change (2020 to the 2030 target) to the GTM 2020 starting point instead of forcing GTM to hit an absolute inventory number. This acknowledges the known mismatch between model-based and inventory-based LULUCF fluxes.
- **An intuitive, decision-relevant result.** Several large Northern-hemisphere forest regions already beat their NDCs under baseline, so their pledges add little. This is a sharp and useful finding for the NDC-enhancement debate.
- **Candid limitations.** The authors list transaction costs, MRV, non-monetary barriers, peatlands and climate impacts openly.

## Key claims

### 1. The NDC scenario was likely never solved as one market equilibrium, which undercuts the paper's own reason for using GTM (load-bearing)

The Methods describe eight scenarios, each with a single carbon price applied to all regions. Fig. 1 then reads each region's flux off these global-price runs and gives each region the lowest price that meets its target. The "NDC scenario" therefore stitches together regions taken from different global equilibria. Brazil's number comes from a world where every region faces $20. North Africa and the Middle East's (AFME) number comes from a world where every region faces $150–200. Japan's comes from a world where everyone faces $50.

The Introduction and Methods argue that GTM is needed precisely because mitigation in one region shifts timber prices and fluxes elsewhere. If the NDC scenario is a patchwork, those cross-region effects are wrong in both directions:
- **Global-price runs overstate output loss in the reference region.** In a $200 global run, every region withdraws timber supply, so prices rise and AFME's own response is distorted.
- **They miss leakage to unpriced or low-priced regions.** In the real NDC world, Group A regions face no price, so leakage would partly offset mitigation bought elsewhere.

CGE analysis of forest conservation reports transnational leakage that can be very large, approaching full offset under plausible trade elasticities (Gan & McCarl 2007). Murray, McCarl & Lee (2004) give program-level estimates. This is exactly the asymmetry that separates the NDC scenario from the Global Coordination scenario, so it bears directly on the "3x for the same money" headline.

**Fix:**
- Run GTM once with region-specific carbon prices set to each region's estimated clearing price (iterating if needed), and report the resulting mitigation, finance and leakage.
- If the patchwork was used deliberately, say so explicitly, and quantify the bias by re-running at least the upper-bound NDC configuration as a heterogeneous-price equilibrium.

### 2. "Finance needed" is defined inconsistently and its timing is ambiguous; the Global Coordination comparison is not like-for-like (load-bearing)

Three problems compound here.

**(a) Transfers versus cost.** Investment is defined as A × Cprice, i.e., the price paid on every tonne of additional mitigation. That is a transfer (programme budget), not the economic cost of abatement (the area under the marginal abatement cost curve).
- Both concepts are legitimate, but the paper uses "costs", "finance" and "investment" interchangeably (Abstract; Results lines 137–153; Discussion lines 180–190).
- Its comparators also mix the two. Turner et al. and FOLU report investment needs, while the UNFCCC SCF report covers country-reported needs.
- Pick one definition, label it consistently, and ideally report both. Integrating under GTM's own regional MAC curves would be straightforward.

**(b) Discounting and dating.** The Methods say annual expenditures are discounted at 5%. The Results call the figures "annual finance in 2030".
- The $76 bn for Global Coordination is reproduced exactly by 4.6 Gt × $20 × 1.03^10 (≈ $26.9/t in 2030) ≈ $124 bn, discounted ten years at 5% (÷1.63) ≈ $76 bn.
- If that is the calculation, the headline numbers are 2020 present values of 2030 payments, not money spent in 2030. The nominal 2030 outlays would be about 60% higher. This matters a great deal for an NCQG-facing paper.
- State the price year and the discounting convention explicitly, and report undiscounted 2030 outlays alongside.
- Likewise, "$20/tCO2" refers to the starting-year price. The 2030 price in that scenario is about $27. Label prices by year throughout, including the low/medium/high bins and Fig. 1.

**(c) The efficiency comparison.** Comparing "4.6 Gt for $76 bn" with "1.6 Gt for $70 bn" mixes two effects:
- the gain from equalising marginal costs;
- the fact that $70 bn is inflated by paying $200 on every AFME tonne.

The cleaner and more standard statistic is the least-cost price and finance needed to deliver the same 1.6 Gt with a uniform price, which GTM can give directly by interpolating between the $5 and $20 runs. Report that, together with the current same-budget comparison. This is the framing Piris-Cabezas et al. and Edmonds et al. (both cited) use for Article 6, and it makes the efficiency claim easy to defend.

The coarse price grid ($5, 20, 35, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200) also inflates regional finance. Each region is charged the next grid price on mitigation that overshoots its target. Interpolate each region's response to find the exact clearing price.

### 3. The Global Coordination scenario sends most "climate finance" to high-income regions and to baseline-additional tonnes; the framing needs to address who pays and what counts (load-bearing for interpretation)

Under Global Coordination, $17 bn goes to the US, Canada, Russia, China, Oceania and rest of South America for 1.6 Gt. Yet the paper frames its results around the NCQG, which concerns flows to developing countries.

Paying Annex I or high-income regions (or Russia) from an international finance pool is a different policy instrument from NCQG finance. It also raises NDC accounting problems: under Article 6, a transferred tonne needs a corresponding adjustment and cannot count toward both the buyer's and the seller's NDC.

The scenario also assumes perfect additionality: payments go only to tonnes beyond the GTM baseline. Real payment-for-ecosystem-services and REDD+ programmes cannot see the counterfactual. Self-selection of already-compliant landholders raises the cost per additional tonne, often a lot (Jack & Jayachandran 2019). This is not a transaction cost in the sense of line 228. It is a wedge between the A × P budget and real programme cost, and it is largest exactly where baselines are uncertain.

**Fixes:**
- Split Global Coordination finance into developing-country and other regions.
- Discuss how this maps onto the NCQG versus Article 6.2/6.4 and domestic policy.
- Add a sensitivity case in which a share of baseline sequestration is also paid for (e.g., 10–30% non-additional payments), to show how robust the efficiency gain is.

The Discussion's statement that high-income Group A countries "can lead by example" (lines 203–205) is the more defensible policy conclusion. Consider moving it up to the Abstract.

### 4. The NDC target construction drives the results and needs validation and sensitivity analysis

- **Coverage.** Table S1 omits the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the largest forest country in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), along with several other large tropical forest nations. Countries without an explicit target are held at constant emissions (lines 321–327). SSA's large Global Coordination response (1.1 Gt) and its near-zero NDC cost both depend on these choices.
  - Show how many SSA, Southeast Asia and South America forest hectares are covered by a target.
  - Test an alternative: apply regional average ambition to uncovered countries.
- **Vintage.** Targets come from Forsell et al. (2016) and den Elzen et al. (2022), which mix INDC-era and 2020–21 updated NDCs. Several key forest countries have revised LULUCF ambition since then. Indonesia's 2022 enhanced NDC is one example: Table S1's −15 to +214 Mt range looks dated for it.
  - Give the NDC vintage used for each country.
  - Say whether the results would change for the five or six countries that dominate the finance totals (Brazil, Indonesia/Southeast Asia, Ethiopia/AFME).
- **Harmonisation.** Applying a GHGI-based Δ to a GTM base year assumes the two systems agree on how fluxes change, even if not on their levels. Grassi et al. (2018, 2022) show the gap is largely definitional: managed-land area and indirect (CO2 fertilisation) sinks counted in inventories but not in bookkeeping models. GTM includes CO2 fertilisation under RCP4.5, and some NDC targets are themselves projections of indirect sinks. So the Δ may mix anthropogenic and environmental components unevenly across countries.
  - Discuss this explicitly.
  - For the largest Group A regions (US, Russia, China), show whether "no finance needed" survives once GTM's CO2-fertilisation contribution to the 2020–2030 sink increase is removed.
- **Table S2.** Clarify whether its values are target fluxes or changes. Western Europe's upper-bound value (−557 Mt) is nearly twice the EU28 entry in Table S1 (−310 Mt), which is confusing as presented.

### 5. The plausibility of 4.6 GtCO2 by 2030 at about $27/t should be pressure-tested against other estimates

The Global Coordination result implies about 4.6 GtCO2/yr of additional forest mitigation within roughly a decade at a 2030 price near $27/t.
- This is close to the lower end of AR6's economic potential for all forests and other ecosystems at prices up to $100/tCO2-eq over 2020–2050 (4.2–7.4 GtCO2-eq/yr; the authors' ref. 10).
- Busch & Engelmann (2017) estimate that $20/t avoids about 41 GtCO2 of tropical deforestation over 35 years, about 1.2 Gt/yr.
- Roe et al. (2021) give country-level cost-effective potentials that can be compared directly by region.

Regional ramp-up constraints matter here. Brown et al. (2019, cited) argue that land-system time lags make such fast responses unlikely. Add a table comparing GTM regional mitigation at $20 and $50 with Roe et al. (2021) country potentials aggregated to GTM regions, and with Busch & Engelmann (2017) for avoided deforestation. Discuss where GTM sits.

Also report the mitigation split by activity (avoided deforestation, improved forest management/rotation, afforestation/reforestation) for each region and scenario. Readers need to know whether the 3x is mostly temperate management responses, which are harder to verify and more exposed to reversal, or tropical avoided deforestation.

## Specific comments

- **Abstract / Discussion lines 193–195.** "Regions accounting for 70% of global forest carbon stocks can meet or exceed their targets with carbon prices less than $100" is never derived in the Results, and is nearly trivial given that every region except AFME clears below $100. Either derive it and give its source, or replace it with a more informative statistic (e.g., the share of total NDC mitigation obtainable below $20 in 2030).
- **Line 47 versus lines 300–301.** The paper cites Roe et al. (2019) for forests making up "more than four-fifths" of land mitigation. Check this against the cost-effective (not technical) potential, since the paper's argument is about cost.
- **Lines 84–86.** The baseline sink of 1.1 GtCO2/yr in 2030 is GTM-defined (FAO-calibrated). It is not comparable to inventory or global carbon budget sinks. Say so, citing Grassi et al. (2022, ref. 34) and Daigneault et al. (2022), who give multi-model baseline ranges including GTM.
- **Lines 112, 140.** Dollar-sign placement ("35$/tCO2", "200$/tCO2") is inconsistent. The units "GT CO2" (line 163) and "Gt CO2" are mixed.
- **Lines 148 and 175.** Fig. 2's caption labels two panels "d". Fig. 2 would read better as a single bar chart of mitigation and finance by region across the three scenarios than as six maps. Maps hide small but expensive regions like Japan and East Asia.
- **Fig. 1.** Show the full regional supply response (flux versus price for all eight prices) instead of binned colours, so readers can see how close each region is to its threshold. Mark the 2030 price for each scenario on the axis.
- **Line 363.** Rental payments for sequestration and subsidies for carbon in products: specify how harvested wood products and substitution are counted in A. Specify whether emissions from deforestation-driven harvest are attributed to the region of harvest.
- **Line 349.** Give the RCP4.5 CO2 fertilisation assumption with SSP2 macro drivers, and its share of the baseline sink increase to 2030 (see Key claim 4).
- **Line 245.** "(UNFCCC 2023)" appears as an author-date citation in a numbered reference list.
- **Line 272.** Give a persistent link or DOI for the GTM code and the scenario files used, and deposit the regional result tables. The current statement points to a generic web page.
- **Ref. 23.** The EDF handbook (2024) should be checked as a source for "theoretical cost-savings via international mitigation transfers". A peer-reviewed source would be stronger.

## Literature and data to engage

- **Roe et al. (2021), "Land-based measures to mitigate climate change: Potential and feasibility by country," *Global Change Biology*.** https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.15873. Country-level cost-effective (<$100) potentials and feasibility; the most direct benchmark for GTM's regional responses (Key claim 5). Also relevant to the "regions with untapped low-cost potential" framing.
- **Grassi et al. (2018), "Reconciling global-model estimates and country reporting of anthropogenic forest CO2 sinks," *Nature Climate Change*.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-018-0283-x. Explains the definitional gap between models and inventories (managed land, indirect effects), which is central to the harmonisation and to the Group A "no finance needed" result.
- **Gan & McCarl (2007), "Measuring transnational leakage of forest conservation," *Ecological Economics* 64(2): 423–432.** https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolec/v64y2007i2p423-432.html. Shows that leakage from unilateral regional forest action can be large. This is why the NDC (regionally heterogeneous) scenario must be solved as a joint equilibrium (Key claim 1).
- **Murray, McCarl & Lee (2004), "Estimating leakage from forest carbon sequestration programs," *Land Economics* 80(1): 109–124.** https://le.uwpress.org/content/80/1/109. Classic leakage estimates for forest carbon programmes; supports the same point.
- **Jack & Jayachandran (2019), "Self-selection into payments for ecosystem services programs," *PNAS* 116(12): 5326–5333.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1802868115. Evidence that real PES programmes pay for non-additional behaviour, so A × P underestimates programme cost (Key claim 3).
- **Busch & Engelmann (2017), "Cost-effectiveness of reducing emissions from tropical deforestation, 2016–2050," *Environmental Research Letters* 13: 015001.** https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/aa907c. Independent tropical MAC curves at $20 and $50 for cross-checking GTM's tropical avoided-deforestation response.
- **Busch et al. (2019), "Potential for low-cost carbon dioxide removal through tropical reforestation," *Nature Climate Change* 9: 463–466.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-019-0485-x. Country-level reforestation MAC curves, including evidence that African reforestation is cheap at $20. Relevant to the large SSA response.
- **Daigneault et al. (2022), "How the future of the global forest sink depends on timber demand, forest management, and carbon policies," *Global Environmental Change* 76: 102582.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2022.102582. A multi-model forest-sector comparison including GTM; use it to place the baseline sink and price responses within a model ensemble and to discuss structural uncertainty.

## Overall assessment

This is a timely, policy-relevant analysis with a clear message. But its two headline numbers, the $28–70 bn NDC cost and the "3x mitigation for 8% more", rest on choices the paper does not yet justify or test:
- a patchwork of global-price runs standing in for a regionally differentiated equilibrium, which neglects the very market feedbacks that motivate using GTM;
- an ambiguous finance metric (transfers versus cost, discounted versus 2030 outlays, starting-year versus 2030 prices);
- an efficiency comparison that is not like-for-like.

All of these can be fixed with the existing model: a heterogeneous-price run, a least-cost run for the same quantity, explicit accounting conventions, and a sensitivity analysis on targets and additionality. They would substantially strengthen the paper.

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The question and the modelling platform are sound, but the central comparisons need to be re-computed and relabelled before the quantitative claims can be relied on. Once revised, it would suit *Nature Communications* or *Nature Climate Change* (Analysis format), or *Global Environmental Change*.
